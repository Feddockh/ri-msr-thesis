\chapter{Conclusions} \label{chapConclusions}

Fire blight costs United States apple growers more than \$100 million a
year, and a single canker that girdles the trunk of a young tree in a
high-density planting can kill it within one
season~\cite{Norelli2003FireBlightManagementTwentyFirst,
Acimovic2023RootstockFireBlight, acimovic2026fireblight}.
The control point for the disease is the dormant season, when the cankers
and shepherd's crooks that carry the pathogen through winter can be found
and pruned out before they seed the following spring's epidemic.
Yet finding them still depends almost entirely on people noticing them
in passing, since dedicating a crew to walk every row of an orchard
looking specifically for symptoms is rarely realistic: more often,
whatever scouting happens is incidental to other field work, such as
spraying, rather than a deliberate search~\cite{Peter2023PennStateExtension}.
That leaves detection slow, inconsistent, and without any spatial
record that could later be used to prioritize pruning crews or
calibrate bloom-period spraying to local risk, and as the agricultural
labor available to growers keeps shrinking, the labor that does exist
needs to go toward the field work itself rather than toward dedicated
disease scouting~\cite{gerlach2025usapple}.

This thesis asked whether a robot could do that job, and answered it by
building one.
The result is an end-to-end system in which close-range multi-modal
sensing, a confidence-aware semantic map, and a disease-aware
next-best-view planner were developed as a single pipeline rather than as
three isolated components, and then deployed and validated on real
symptomatic trees in a working orchard.
Its central finding is that deciding where to look next is worth as much
as looking more carefully: a planner that spends its viewpoints resolving
uncertainty about disease, rather than uncertainty about geometry,
reaches detection quality comparable to a complete fixed scan in roughly
half the viewpoints and half the time.

\section{Summary of Contributions} \label{secContribSummary}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Multi-modal orchard dataset.}
  We assembled 1{,}275 flash-illuminated stereo RGB images of dormant
  apple trees, each paired with a spatially registered 25-band NIR frame,
  and annotated them for the two dormant-season symptom
  classes---shepherd's crook and canker---under a protocol developed with
  a plant pathologist, yielding 1{,}000 crook and 1{,}302 canker
  instances.
  Hand-labeling was made tractable by a SAM2-assisted box-to-mask tool
  and a geometric cross-modal transfer procedure that propagates RGB
  annotations onto the corresponding NIR frames.
  The data are partitioned into leakage-free train, validation, and test
  splits by embedding similarity rather than by frame index, with the
  most similar cross-split image pair reaching only 0.916, so that
  reported performance reflects generalization rather than memorization.
  To our knowledge this is the first dataset pairing dense NIR spectral
  bands with RGB imagery of dormant woody tissue
  (Chapter~\ref{chapDataset}).

  \item \textbf{2D fire blight detection pipeline.}
  YOLO26-L instance segmentation models trained separately on each
  modality reach a test mAP@0.5 of 0.605 for RGB and 0.565 for NIR, with
  the RGB model ahead on every metric.
  We attribute that gap to an initialization asymmetry rather than to the
  modality itself: the RGB model inherits COCO-pretrained low-level
  features, whereas the NIR model must learn its entire feature hierarchy
  from 983 training images.
  A failure-mode analysis further showed that much of the apparent error
  is boundary ambiguity rather than missed detection, since relaxing the
  matching threshold to IoU~$\geq 0.3$ raises test mAP to 0.734 and 0.703
  respectively on predictions a human would already judge correct.
  This finding is what motivated aggregating evidence across many views
  rather than trusting any single frame (Chapter~\ref{chapDataset}).

  \item \textbf{Semantic OctoMap for disease mapping.}
  We introduced a volumetric representation in which every voxel carries
  an occupancy probability, a disease class label, and a fused
  confidence, with semantic labels attached only to voxels already
  established as occupied surfaces.
  A boost-and-penalty fusion rule reinforces mutually consistent
  observations toward high confidence while driving contested ones down,
  which gives the map a self-correcting character: later, better views
  override earlier detection errors, and the voxels left at low
  confidence explicitly mark the regions that warrant another look
  (Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}).

  \item \textbf{Semantic Next-Best-View planner.}
  Rather than scoring candidate views by geometric unknown alone, the
  semantic planner scores them by the disease-label uncertainty they
  would resolve, concentrating its attention on contested symptomatic
  regions.
  In simulation it attained the best detection performance of the three
  planners at a 30-viewpoint budget, reaching an mAP of 0.479 and a
  max-F1 of 0.588 against 0.366 and 0.465 for the deterministic baseline,
  while the volumetric planner attained the most complete geometric
  coverage at 82.03\%.
  This division of labor---the volumetric planner best at mapping space,
  the semantic planner best at finding disease---is consistent with what
  each planner is asked to optimize, and it held in both settings
  (Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}).

  \item \textbf{Full-system field validation.}
  Erwin integrates these components onto an Amiga base and an xArm6
  manipulator, with all perception, mapping, and planning running
  onboard, and was deployed on 12 dormant apple trees at the Penn State
  Fruit Research and Extension Center under a genuine train--test domain
  gap.
  The field results reproduced the simulation findings and sharpened the
  central one: at viewpoint 15 the semantic planner more than doubled the
  baseline's mAP, 0.209 against 0.089, and nearly quadrupled its max-F1,
  0.461 against 0.124, reaching detection quality comparable to a
  complete baseline scan in roughly half the viewpoints and half the
  inspection time.
  Two supporting results made the deployment possible: a FoundationStereo
  configuration optimized from 3.8\,s to 32\,ms per frame, and a
  Gaussian-splat ground-truth pipeline that places expert 3D symptom
  annotations directly into the robot's own frame
  (Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations} \label{secLimitations}

The system works, but it is a research prototype, and the distance between
what it demonstrates and what a grower could actually use is worth stating
plainly.
The limitations below are framed from the perspective of that end user.

\paragraph{Inspection speed.}
The most immediate barrier to practical use is throughput.
A complete 30-viewpoint inspection takes 3--5 minutes, and the base must
then be repositioned by an operator before the next tree, so even a single
row of a few dozen trees becomes a multi-hour job and a commercial block
is out of reach within a working day.
The bottleneck is mechanical rather than computational: with stereo depth
running at 32\,ms and detection running alongside it in parallel
(Section~\ref{secStereoDepth}), the cycle is dominated by the time the arm
spends moving between viewpoints.
The semantic planner's central result---comparable map accuracy in roughly
half the viewpoints---is the first meaningful step against this limit,
since halving the viewpoint count halves the motion, but it is a
mitigation rather than a solution.

\paragraph{Cost and durability.}
Erwin is expensive, and it is not ruggedized.
The xArm6 is a general-purpose industrial manipulator designed for factory
floors rather than orchards, and the computing enclosure is not
weather-sealed; neither would survive sustained rain, and wind moving the
canopy between views would undermine the static-scene assumption the map
depends on.
The system is therefore a fair-weather instrument, which is an awkward
constraint for a task whose entire window falls in late winter and early
spring, precisely when orchard weather is least cooperative.
Its capital cost, meanwhile, is well above what an individual grower
operating on the margins described in Chapter~\ref{chapIntroduction} could
justify.

\paragraph{Operational complexity.}
Running the system today means bringing up and coordinating a set of
concurrent ROS 2 nodes---camera driver, detector, stereo matcher, mapping,
planning, and manipulator control---and diagnosing them individually when
one misbehaves.
That demands an operator fluent in the software stack rather than an
orchard employee, and it is the clearest sense in which this remains a
prototype rather than a product.
None of it is fundamental, and the awkwardness is in part a deliberate
trade: the modular decomposition that makes the stack demanding to operate
is the same decomposition that allowed detectors and planners to be
swapped freely during development (Section~\ref{secROS2}).

\paragraph{Detection accuracy.}
Map accuracy remains bounded by detector accuracy.
The planners can only refine and localize the symptoms the 2D detector is
able to recognize in the first place, so the field mAP of 0.322 at
viewpoint 30 reflects the detector's ceiling as much as the planner's
behavior.
Multi-view fusion recovers a great deal of what any single frame misses,
which is precisely why the mapping and planning contributions exist, but a
grower would want a system that misses fewer symptoms outright, and
substantial headroom remains.

\section{Future Work} \label{secFutureWork}

The limitations above suggest a fairly direct agenda.

\subsection{Improved Detection Models} \label{secFutureDetection}

Because detection bounds everything downstream, it is the most valuable
place to improve, and two directions follow from what
Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} already established.

The first is to use both imaging modalities at once.
The spectral analysis of Section~\ref{secSpectralAnalysis} confirmed that
the red-edge and near-infrared region carries information that
discriminates dormant fire blight, and the platform already carries the
multispectral camera through every inspection, yet the closed-loop system
uses the RGB modality alone and the two detectors were trained and
evaluated in isolation from each other.
A multi-modal model would combine the complementary strengths that
comparison exposed: the pretrained low-level features that account for
most of the RGB model's advantage, and the NIR sensitivity to
physiological changes that accompany infection before they become obvious
in visible light.
That the NIR detector draws essentially level with RGB on cankers once
boundary ambiguity is discounted, at 0.595 against 0.594 mAP@0.3, despite
learning from scratch on fewer than a thousand images, suggests the
modality is underused rather than uninformative.

The second is to move from bounding boxes toward semantic segmentation.
The failure-mode analysis of Section~\ref{secDetectionResults} showed that
boxes are a poor primitive for symptoms that stretch irregularly along a
branch with no clear start or end: predictions land on the right tissue
and are scored as failures anyway because their boxes do not align with an
equally arbitrary ground-truth box.
Segmentation would describe a symptom's actual extent and would feed the
semantic map more precisely than a box can.
The obstacle is annotation cost, since segmentation labels are far slower
to produce by hand, and the model that would make labeling fast is the
very thing being trained.
This is the same circularity the SAM2 tool of Appendix~\ref{apxSAM2}
already breaks once, by annotating a first batch interactively,
fine-tuning on that batch, and using the improved model to accelerate the
rest; the same fine-tune-then-automate loop is the practical path to a
segmentation dataset at scale.

\subsection{Faster and Lighter-Weight Sensor Positioning}
\label{secFutureManipulation}

The speed, cost, and durability limitations share a single root: the
manipulator.
A 6-DOF industrial arm is a heavy, slow, and expensive way to move a
camera weighing a small fraction of what the arm is rated to carry, and
replacing it with a lighter, faster, purpose-built positioning mechanism
would attack all three limitations at once.
What makes this an attractive direction rather than a rebuild is that the
planning contribution does not depend on the mechanism.
The planner requires only an action space of camera poses that are
reachable and collision-free, expressed through the precomputed workspace
model of Section~\ref{secWorkspaceFilter}; how those poses are physically
attained is immaterial to the map, to the information-gain formulation,
and to the selection loop.
A new positioning mechanism would therefore inherit the semantic planner
unchanged, and would convert the planner's half-the-viewpoints result
directly into a proportionally faster inspection.

\subsection{Orchard-Scale Autonomy} \label{secFuturePlanning}

Erwin inspects one tree at a time from a stationary base, and an operator
drives it to the next.
Closing that gap with autonomous row-to-row traversal, so that the
platform positions itself in front of each tree, inspects it, and moves
on, is the most direct path from a per-tree capability to the
orchard-scale system the problem actually calls for.

It is also what would make broader evaluation tractable.
The field validation of Chapter~\ref{chapErwin} covered 12 trees at a
single site within one visit, bounded both by the need to run three
planners on every tree and by the scarcity of standing fire blight, and
characterizing generalization properly requires far more trees, across
more orchards, varieties, and seasons than a hand-driven platform can
reach.
Autonomy and evidence scale together here: the same capability that makes
the system useful to a grower is what would let its claims be tested at
the scale they deserve.

Beyond traversal, the per-tree maps this system already produces are the
raw material for the orchard-wide severity map that current practice lacks
(Section~\ref{secFireBlightCurrentSolutions}).
Georeferencing each tree's symptom count and confidence into a block-level
record is what would let a grower direct pruning crews toward the worst
rows and calibrate bloom-period spray intensity to measured local risk
rather than to a uniform assumption---the decisions that motivated this
work in the first place.

\subsection{Richer Map Representations} \label{secFutureMapping}

The maps in this thesis are single-visit snapshots, but fire blight is a
process that unfolds across seasons.
Because the canker load standing at the end of one dormant season predicts
the disease pressure of the next
(Section~\ref{secFireBlightMechanism}), a map that persists across visits
would be worth considerably more than the sum of its snapshots: a robot
returning to the same trees could track whether an infection is
progressing, confirm that a pruning crew removed what was prescribed, and
accumulate the longitudinal record that manual scouting has never
produced.
The technical prerequisite is registration across visits, relating this
season's map to last season's on a canopy that has both grown and been
cut, and the splat alignment machinery of Appendix~\ref{apxSuperSplat},
built here to place ground truth into the robot's frame, is a natural
starting point for it.

\subsection{From Detection to Treatment} \label{secFutureManagement}

The natural end of this line of work is a system that does something about
what it finds.
Detection and treatment are separated today by a gap that is largely
mechanical rather than informational, because the semantic map already
supplies what an intervention needs: the 3D locations of symptoms, with
confidences, in the robot's own frame.
Standard practice is already to cut well below the visible margin of a
canker (Section~\ref{secFireBlightCurrentSolutions}), so a map of canker
locations is close to a pruning prescription; targeted spraying and
germicidal UV treatment are likewise interventions that must be aimed,
which is exactly what the map provides.
An inspection pass that ends in treatment rather than in a report would
close the loop from sensing to action, and would make the case for the
robot to a grower far more directly than a map alone.

\section{Closing Remarks} \label{secClosingRemarks}

The purpose of this work is not, in the end, a better detector or a
cleverer planner.
It is to give a grower a fighting chance against a disease that can take a
young block in a single season.
A high-density planting represents years of capital committed before the
first full harvest, and fire blight turns that investment into a wager on
how much a person can see walking down a row.
A robot that inspects every tree, records what it finds where it found it,
and hands back a map is not a cure.
It is something more modest and more useful: the difference between
managing a disease from evidence and managing it from impression.
Yields become more predictable, interventions land where they are
actually needed, and a bad year becomes less likely to be a ruinous one.

That case does not stop at the orchard gate.
Apple is a globally significant crop, fire blight now occurs in more than
sixty countries, and the labor available to scout for it is shrinking
nearly everywhere at once.
Losses to plant disease are, ultimately, losses of food, and tools that
prevent them---applied earlier, more precisely, and with less
labor---compound into a food supply that is both larger and steadier than
it would otherwise be.
This thesis contributes one piece of that: the demonstration that a robot
can look at a tree, decide for itself where to look next, and come away
knowing where the disease is.
