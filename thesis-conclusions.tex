\chapter{Conclusions} \label{chapConclusions}

Fire blight costs United States apple growers more than \$100 million a
year, and a single canker that girdles the trunk of a young tree in a
high-density planting can kill it within one
season~\cite{Norelli2003FireBlightManagementTwentyFirst,
Acimovic2023RootstockFireBlight, acimovic2026fireblight}.
The control point for the disease is the dormant season, when the cankers
and shepherd's crooks that carry the pathogen through winter can be found
and pruned out before they seed the following spring's epidemic.
Yet finding them still depends almost entirely on people noticing them
in passing, since dedicating a crew to walk every row of an orchard
looking specifically for symptoms is rarely realistic: more often,
whatever scouting happens is incidental to other field work, such as
spraying, rather than a deliberate search~\cite{Peter2023PennStateExtension}.
That leaves detection slow, inconsistent, and without any spatial
record that could later be used to prioritize pruning crews or
calibrate bloom-period spraying to local risk, and as the agricultural
labor available to growers keeps shrinking, the labor that does exist
needs to go toward the field work itself rather than toward dedicated
disease scouting~\cite{gerlach2025usapple}.

This thesis addresses that need by developing and demonstrating an
autonomous robotic system for orchard disease scouting.
The result is an end-to-end system in which close-range multi-modal
sensing, a confidence-aware semantic map, and a disease-aware
next-best-view planner were developed as a single pipeline rather than as
three isolated components, and then deployed and validated on real
symptomatic trees in a working orchard.
Its central finding is that deciding where to look next is worth as much
as looking more carefully: a planner that spends its viewpoints resolving
uncertainty about disease, rather than uncertainty about geometry, more
than doubles the detection accuracy of a complete fixed scan at the halfway
point of the inspection.

\section{Future Work} \label{secFutureWork}

The clearest next steps follow directly from where the system falls
short of what a grower could put to work today: detection accuracy still
bounds map accuracy, per-tree inspection is too slow for a commercial
block, and the planner itself is still a hand-engineered score rather
than a learned policy.

\subsection{Improved Detection Models} \label{secFutureDetection}

Map accuracy remains bounded by detector accuracy: the planners can only
refine and localize the symptoms the 2D detector is able to recognize in
the first place, so the field mAP of 0.322 at viewpoint 30
(Section~\ref{secFieldResults}) reflects the detector's ceiling as much
as the planner's behavior, and closing that gap is the single most
valuable place to improve the system. Two directions follow from what
Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} already established.

The first is to use both imaging modalities at once.
The spectral analysis of Section~\ref{secSpectralAnalysis} confirmed that
the red-edge and near-infrared region carries information that
discriminates dormant fire blight, and the platform already carries the
multispectral camera through every inspection, yet the closed-loop system
uses the RGB modality alone and the two detectors were trained and
evaluated in isolation from each other.
A multi-modal model would combine the complementary strengths that
comparison exposed: the pretrained low-level features and higher native
spatial resolution that account for most of the RGB model's advantage,
and the NIR sensitivity to physiological changes that accompany infection
before they become obvious in visible light.
That the NIR detector draws essentially level with RGB on cankers once
boundary ambiguity is discounted, at 0.595 against 0.594 mAP@0.3, despite
learning from scratch on fewer than a thousand images, suggests the
modality is underused rather than uninformative.

The second is to move from bounding boxes toward semantic segmentation.
The failure-mode analysis of Section~\ref{secDetectionResults} showed that
boxes are a poor primitive for symptoms that stretch irregularly along a
branch with no clear start or end: predictions land on the right tissue
and are scored as failures anyway because their boxes do not align with an
equally arbitrary ground-truth box.
Segmentation would describe a symptom's actual extent and would feed the
semantic map more precisely than a box can.
The obstacle is annotation cost, since segmentation labels are far slower
to produce by hand, and the model that would make labeling fast is the
very thing being trained.
This is the same circularity the SAM2 tool of Appendix~\ref{apxSAM2}
already breaks once, by annotating a first batch interactively,
fine-tuning on that batch, and using the improved model to accelerate the
rest; the same fine-tune-then-automate loop is the practical path to a
segmentation dataset at scale.

\subsection{Faster Sensor Positioning and Continuous Detection}
\label{secFutureManipulation}

Inspection speed is the most immediate barrier to practical use.
A complete 30-viewpoint inspection takes 3--5 minutes, so even with the
platform's autonomous row-to-row navigation moving the base between trees,
a single row of a few dozen trees becomes a multi-hour job and a
commercial block is out of reach within a working day.
The bottleneck is mechanical rather than computational: with stereo depth
running at 32\,ms and detection running alongside it in parallel
(Section~\ref{secStereoDepth}), the cycle is dominated by the time the arm
spends moving between viewpoints and stopping to capture at each one.
The semantic planner's front-loading of detection quality is a first
meaningful step against this limit, but it is a mitigation rather than a
solution.

Two changes attack this bottleneck directly, and can be pursued together.
The first is a lighter, faster, purpose-built positioning mechanism in
place of the 6-DOF xArm6, which is a heavy, slow, general-purpose
industrial arm rated to carry far more than the sensor rig weighs.
This is an attractive direction rather than a rebuild because the
planning contribution does not depend on the mechanism: the planner
requires only an action space of camera poses that are reachable and
collision-free, expressed through the precomputed workspace model of
Section~\ref{secWorkspaceFilter}, so a new positioning mechanism would
inherit the semantic planner unchanged.
The second is continuous detection: the rig and perception pipeline
already support a sustained capture rate of 20\,fps, verified in the lab
(Section~\ref{secPerceptionPipeline}), but the field experiments
captured a single image at each selected viewpoint rather than streaming
continuously, so that planner comparisons were not confounded by
incidental images gathered along the arm's in-transit motion.
Outside of a controlled comparison, that constraint does not apply:
fusing detections continuously while the arm is in motion, rather than
stopping to capture at each selected viewpoint, would let a faster
positioning mechanism convert its speed directly into inspection time.
Together, a lighter mechanism and continuous detection are the most
direct path from a few minutes to a few seconds per tree.

\subsection{Learning the Information Gain Function} \label{secFutureLearnedPolicy}

The planner presented in Chapter~\ref{chapNBV} scores candidate views
with a hand-engineered utility function that combines semantic and
occupancy uncertainty, and a hand-designed score can only combine the
terms its authors thought to include.
A learned alternative would replace that formula with a network trained
to predict how much a candidate view will actually improve the map,
using the observed change in map confidence after each real viewpoint
is captured as the training signal, rather than a fixed uncertainty
heuristic as a proxy for that improvement.
Since this only replaces the scoring step, the rest of the pipeline,
including candidate generation, workspace filtering, and greedy
selection, would carry over unchanged, and the existing field and
simulation rollouts already provide the map-before-and-after pairs
needed to train it.

\section{Closing Remarks} \label{secClosingRemarks}

The purpose of this work is not, in the end, a better detector or a
cleverer planner.
It is to give a grower a fighting chance against a disease that can take a
young block in a single season.
A high-density planting represents years of capital committed before the
first full harvest, and fire blight turns that investment into a wager on
whether someone happens to notice a canker before it spreads.
A robot that inspects every tree, records what it finds where it found it,
and hands back a map is not a cure.
It is something more modest: the difference between managing a disease
from evidence and managing it from impression.
Yields become more predictable, interventions land where they are
actually needed, and a bad year becomes less likely to be a ruinous one.

That case does not stop at the orchard gate.
Apple is a globally significant crop, fire blight now occurs in more than
sixty countries, and the labor available to scout for it is shrinking
nearly everywhere at once.
Losses to plant disease are, ultimately, losses of food, and tools that
prevent them (applied earlier, more precisely, and with less
labor) compound into a food supply that is both larger and steadier than
it would otherwise be.
This thesis contributes one piece of that: the demonstration that a robot
can look at a tree, decide for itself where to look next, and come away
knowing where the disease is.
