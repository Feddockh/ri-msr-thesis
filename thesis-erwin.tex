\chapter{Integrated System and Field Validation} \label{chapErwin}

The preceding chapters developed the pieces of an autonomous
disease-inspection system in isolation: a multi-modal dataset and 2D
detector (Chapter~\ref{chapDataset}), and a confidence-aware semantic map
with a family of next-best-view planners evaluated in simulation
(Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}). This chapter describes how those pieces are
integrated into a single physical system---Erwin---and reports its
validation on real dormant apple trees in a working orchard. Erwin combines
an Amiga mobile base, a UFACTORY xArm6 manipulator, and the multi-modal
sensor rig into a closed perception--action loop that runs entirely onboard:
the arm positions the camera, images are captured and processed into
detections, the detections update the semantic OctoMap, and the NBV planner
selects the next viewpoint, repeating until the inspection budget is
exhausted. Erwin was developed as part of Carnegie Mellon University's Fire
Blighters entry in the 2026 Farm Robotics Challenge, where the team received
the Amiga Innovation Award.

Section~\ref{secRobotPlatform} describes the physical platform and its
sensor integration; Section~\ref{secSystemArch} describes the onboard
software architecture and the runtime perception and planning loop;
Section~\ref{secFieldExperiments} reports the field experiments, in which
all three planners of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV} were deployed and evaluated on
12 dormant apple trees; and Section~\ref{secFieldDiscussion} discusses what
the field results reveal about the system and about the transfer from
simulation to the orchard.

\section{Robot Platform} \label{secRobotPlatform}

Erwin is a mobile manipulation system designed for autonomous disease
inspection in orchard rows. A 6-DOF xArm6 manipulator is mounted on
horizontal rails atop an Amiga mobile base, which provides locomotion
between trees, and a custom camera rig is rigidly fixed to the manipulator
end effector. During inspection of a given tree the base remains stationary
while the arm actively repositions the sensor, breaking the plane of the
canopy, navigating around occluding branches, and acquiring the close,
oriented views that the narrow-field flash and multispectral cameras
require. Figure~\ref{figRobotPlatform} shows the assembled platform deployed
in a dormant orchard.

\subsection{Mobile Base: Amiga} \label{secAmiga}

The mobile base is a Farm-ng Amiga, an open, electrically driven
agricultural platform whose narrow footprint and modular mounting rails suit
the constrained geometry of an orchard row
(Section~\ref{secAgRobotics}). In this work the Amiga carries the
manipulator, sensor rig, and onboard compute between trees and provides a
stable, level platform from which the arm operates; it remains fixed in
front of a tree throughout that tree's inspection, so that all viewpoint
variation comes from the arm rather than the base. Autonomous row-to-row
traversal, which would let the base reposition itself along a full row
without an operator, is outside the scope of this thesis and is identified
as a priority for future work (Chapter~\ref{chapConclusions}).

\subsection{Manipulator: xArm6} \label{secXArm6}

The manipulator is a UFACTORY xArm6, a 6-DOF arm mounted on horizontal rails
on the Amiga. Its six degrees of freedom allow the camera rig to be
positioned and oriented freely enough to break the plane of the canopy and
look around occluding branches, which is the physical capability that active
viewpoint selection depends on: without an arm able to reach oblique,
in-canopy poses, the planners of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV} would have no
meaningful action space. The reachable workspace of the arm, sampled
offline, defines the feasible viewpoint set $\mathcal{Q}$ used for candidate
filtering (Section~\ref{secWorkspaceFilter}), and collision-aware motion
planning ensures that executed viewpoints avoid contact with the tree and
the platform.

\subsection{Sensor Integration and Calibration} \label{secSensorCalib}

The camera rig fixed to the end effector houses the flash-illuminated stereo
RGB camera and the 25-band NIR multispectral camera introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chapDataset}; the multispectral camera is carried on the
platform but is not used in the closed-loop inspection experiments reported
here, which rely on the flash stereo RGB modality. Because the planners
reason in a single global frame, every sensor observation must be expressed
in that frame, which requires both intrinsic calibration of each camera and
extrinsic calibration of the rig relative to the end effector. Intrinsic
parameters (focal length, principal point, and distortion) and the stereo
baseline are those reported in Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}, and stereo
frames are undistorted and rectified before depth estimation. At runtime,
the fixed rig-to-end-effector transform is composed with the manipulator's
forward kinematics and the base pose to place each semantic point cloud into
the common global frame used by the map (Section~\ref{secProjection}).

Each camera's intrinsics were calibrated using the ROS 2 camera calibration
tool with a ChArUco board, which combines the corner-localization precision
of a chessboard pattern with the per-square ArUco markers needed to identify
board pose even when the board is partially occluded or only partially in
view, as in Figure~\ref{figCameraCalib}. Calibration was repeated several
times for each camera, and a calibration was accepted only once the
resulting reprojection error fell below $1.0$ pixel, so that the intrinsic
parameters used throughout this work are consistently sub-pixel accurate.

\section{System Architecture} \label{secSystemArch}

All perception, mapping, and planning run onboard a desktop-grade computing
unit with an RTX 4070 GPU, under ROS 2 middleware, which coordinates
sensing, mapping, planning, and control. The system operates as the closed
perception--action loop already formalized in Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}: stereo
images are processed into semantic 3D reconstructions, candidate viewpoints
are generated from the current map state and detection uncertainty, and the
manipulator is commanded to the selected viewpoint, with all sensor data
transformed into a common global frame using robot odometry and manipulator
kinematics.

\subsection{ROS 2 Integration} \label{secROS2}

The software is organized as a ROS 2 node graph in which each stage of the
pipeline is a separate node communicating over topics and services. The
principal nodes are the stereo camera driver, which issues the hardware
trigger and publishes synchronized left and right frames; the detection
node, which runs the YOLO-based instance segmentation model of
Section~\ref{sec2DDetection} on each frame; the depth node, which runs the
FoundationStereo matcher (Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}); the mapping node,
which fuses the resulting semantic point cloud into the semantic OctoMap
(Section~\ref{secSemanticOctoMap}); the planning node, which implements the
NBV selection loop of Algorithm~\ref{algNBV}; and the manipulator control
node, which plans and executes collision-free motions to the selected
viewpoint. This modular decomposition is what allows the perception
front-end to be swapped---for example, between the simulation-fine-tuned and
real-image detector variants of Section~\ref{secDetectionResults}---without
altering the mapping or planning stages.

\subsection{Perception Pipeline} \label{secPerceptionPipeline}

At runtime, the perception pipeline transforms each captured stereo pair
into an incremental update of the semantic OctoMap through the four stages
introduced in Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} and formalized in
Section~\ref{secProjection}: flash-illuminated stereo acquisition,
instance-level detection, depth estimation, and semantic point cloud
construction. A hardware trigger synchronizes the flash pulse with the
stereo exposure so that left and right frames are captured under identical,
controlled illumination. The captured pair is passed in parallel to the
detector, which produces per-instance masks, class labels, and confidences,
and to the FoundationStereo matcher, optimized for real-time inference as
described in Section~\ref{secStereoDepth}. The detection masks
are rasterized into per-pixel class and confidence maps, each valid pixel is
back-projected using the stereo depth and assigned its class and confidence,
and the resulting semantic point cloud is integrated into the map; the
stages of this pipeline are the ones illustrated in
Figure~\ref{figProjectionPipeline}, and the projection and fusion steps are
exactly those of Section~\ref{secProjection}.

The rig and pipeline together support a maximum sustained capture rate of
$20$\,fps, achieved through the stereo optimizations of
Section~\ref{secStereoDepth} together with running detection and depth
estimation in parallel.
We separately tested a continuous $5$\,fps feed and found that it
reconstructed the scene substantially faster than single-image capture,
since the map received many more observations per unit time. For the planner
evaluation reported below, however, this continuous mode was deliberately
disabled: all three planners were run with a single triggered image captured
at each viewpoint, so that differences in performance reflect the planning
strategy alone and are not confounded by capture rate.

\subsection{Real-Time Stereo Depth in the Field} \label{secStereoDepth}

One capability that simulation provides for free must be earned in the
field: depth. The simulated camera of Section~\ref{secOrchardSim} renders
an exact depth image alongside each RGB frame, but the physical rig must
recover depth from its stereo pair, and the quality of that recovery
bounds everything downstream---a branch whose disparity is wrong is a
branch the map places incorrectly and the planner then reasons about
incorrectly. Dormant canopies are close to a worst case for stereo
matching, dominated by thin, repetitive structures separated by large
depth discontinuities. Classical block-based methods such as semi-global
block matching (SGBM)~\cite{hirschmuller2008sgm} struggle in exactly this
regime, as Figure~\ref{figDisparityComparison} shows on a simulated stereo
pair: SGBM produces speckled, blocky disparity with large invalid regions
along thin branches, while FoundationStereo
\cite{wen2025foundationstereozeroshotstereomatching}, a learned matcher
that ranked first on the Middlebury benchmark
\cite{scharstein2002middlebury} at the time of development, recovers
smooth, complete disparity down to the thinnest structures. We therefore
adopted FoundationStereo, and the engineering question became whether a
state-of-the-art learned matcher could be made fast enough for a closed
perception--action loop.

The final input resolution is low, but deliberately so. Dense stereo point
clouds are routinely subsampled with a pixel stride before insertion into
a volumetric map, because the map's fixed voxel size cannot represent the
extra density anyway, so computing an expensive full-resolution cloud only
to discard most of it is wasted work. Running the matcher at $448\times224$
and keeping every point yields a cloud of comparable effective density to
a strided full-resolution one, while leaving the GPU largely free for the
detector running in parallel.

One artifact required attention. FoundationStereo tends to leave trailing
pixels at depth discontinuities, where foreground disparity smears past
the edge of a branch, and these back-project into streaks of floating
points around the canopy (Figure~\ref{figSpeckleFilter}). The reference
implementation removes such points with a k-nearest-neighbor outlier
filter in 3D, which is effective but far too slow for the closed loop. We
instead run a speckle filter directly on the disparity map, which removes
small disconnected disparity regions in 2D at negligible cost and
suppresses the majority of the stray points before they are ever
back-projected.

\subsection{Planning and Control Loop} \label{secPlanningControl}

Once the base is positioned in front of a tree, inspection proceeds as a
fixed cycle, executing the NBV selection loop of Algorithm~\ref{algNBV} at
each step:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the mapping node integrates the most recent image into the semantic
        OctoMap;
  \item the planning node generates candidate viewpoints from the current
        map, filters them against the reachable workspace and collision
        constraints (Section~\ref{secWorkspaceFilter}), scores the feasible
        candidates by the utility of Equation~\ref{eqUtility}, and selects
        the maximum-utility viewpoint;
  \item the manipulator control node plans and executes a collision-free
        motion to that viewpoint;
  \item the stereo camera captures a triggered image at the new pose,
        closing the loop.
\end{enumerate}
The cycle repeats until the viewpoint budget is exhausted. Candidates are
evaluated in descending order of utility until one yields a feasible motion
plan, so that the arm always moves to the best reachable view rather than
stalling on an unreachable optimum. A complete 30-viewpoint inspection takes
on the order of 3--5 minutes to execute (Section~\ref{secFieldResults}).

\section{Field Experiments} \label{secFieldExperiments}

To test whether the simulation findings of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV} hold under
real conditions, Erwin was deployed in a working orchard and used to inspect
real dormant apple trees with each of the three planners. The field
experiments serve two purposes: to validate that the full integrated system
operates end-to-end and autonomously in the field, and to compare the
planners under the domain shift, occlusion, and lighting variability that
simulation cannot fully reproduce.

\subsection{Experimental Site} \label{secFieldSite}

Field evaluation was conducted at the Penn State Fruit Research and
Extension Center in Adams County, Pennsylvania, from March 18 to March 20,
2026, using 12 dormant apple trees, of which 9 exhibited fire blight
symptoms and 3 appeared healthy; Figure~\ref{figFieldSite} maps the study
trees within the orchard block, and the site's attributes are summarized
alongside the two image-collection orchards in
Table~\ref{tabCollectionSites}. The three healthy trees provide
true-negative cases, testing whether the planners and detector avoid
hallucinating symptoms on disease-free canopy---a case that pooled detection
metrics explicitly penalize (Section~\ref{secSimSetup}). This site is
deliberately distinct from the commercial orchard whose imagery was used to
train the detector (Section~\ref{secDetectionResults}): no images from the
evaluation site were used during training, so the field results measure
performance under a genuine train--test domain gap. The number of trees was
limited to 12 for two practical reasons: each tree had to be inspected by
all three planners within a single multi-day site visit, and symptomatic
trees are inherently scarce, since growers prune out fire blight infections
as soon as they are found, leaving few orchards with standing disease
available for study.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol} \label{secFieldProtocol}

Each of the 12 trees was inspected by all three planners---baseline grid
scan, volumetric NBV, and semantic NBV---so that the planners are compared
on identical trees under matched conditions. For every tree, the base was
positioned $1.0$\,m from the trunk to fix the sensing geometry, matching the
standoff used in simulation, and a $1.0 \times 1.0 \times 2.0$\,m bounding
box was fitted to the tree to define a consistent ROI
(Section~\ref{secSimSetup}). Each planner was run for a 30-viewpoint budget,
and detection quality and coverage were recorded at every viewpoint using
the metrics of Section~\ref{secSimSetup}.
Every run began with the same four fixed initialization viewpoints,
counted within the 30-viewpoint budget, before the planner's own selection
strategy took over; the collision-avoidance motivation for this
initialization is described in Section~\ref{secFieldQualitative}.
For comparability, the simulation experiments of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}
were evaluated under the same four-viewpoint initialization, which is why
the per-viewpoint curves of all three planners coincide over the first few
viewpoints in both settings.

Ground truth for each field tree was obtained by reconstructing a 3D
Gaussian splat of the tree and manually annotating its symptomatic regions,
described in Section~\ref{secGSSplat} below. As in simulation, all three
planners were evaluated with single-image-per-viewpoint capture rather than
the continuous $5$\,fps feed described in
Section~\ref{secPerceptionPipeline}. The continuous feed was tested
separately and reconstructed scenes faster, but it was excluded from the
planner comparison because it would conflate capture rate with planning
strategy; holding capture fixed at one image per viewpoint ensures the
comparison isolates the effect of the planner.

\subsection{Ground Truth Generation with Gaussian Splatting} \label{secGSSplat}

Quantitative evaluation of the field deployment requires a reference set of
true symptom locations against which the map's detections can be scored,
expressed as 3D centroids so that the same evaluation procedure
(Section~\ref{secSimSetup}) used in simulation (Section~\ref{secSimGT})
applies unchanged to real trees. Because no exact mesh exists for a real
tree, ground truth is instead reconstructed photogrammetrically, as a 3D
Gaussian splat~\cite{kerbl20233dgs} of each tree that is annotated in 3D
and registered into the robot's frame.

The reconstruction proceeds as follows. Prior to each inspection, a video
orbit of the tree was captured by walking around it with an iPhone 15 Pro.
Frames extracted from the video were passed to
COLMAP~\cite{schoenberger2016sfm} to recover camera poses, using its
default SIFT features and, for the more challenging reconstructions,
SuperPoint features~\cite{detone2018superpoint} matched with
LightGlue~\cite{lindenberger2023lightglue}. The posed images were then
used to train a Gaussian splat with the Splatfacto method of
Nerfstudio~\cite{tancik2023nerfstudio}. A second splat of the same tree
was built by the same pipeline from the flash stereo images captured
during the robot's inspection run, providing a second, flash-illuminated
view of the same canopy.

Both splats must then be expressed in the robot's frame before annotations
made on them can serve as ground truth for the robot's map. We performed
this registration, and the subsequent annotation, in a modified version of
the SuperSplat editor developed for this work and described in
Appendix~\ref{apxSuperSplat}. The stereo-fused point cloud
accumulated by the robot during the run, which is natively in the robot's
frame, is loaded alongside each splat, and four or more corresponding
points are selected on the two models; the tool solves for the similarity
transform that aligns the splat to the point cloud and reports the
residual alignment error. With both splats registered, symptomatic regions
are segmented directly on the splat by selecting and labeling its
vertices, with guidance from domain experts and switching between the
phone and flash modalities where either is ambiguous. The centroid of each
annotated region, computed by the tool and exported in the robot frame, is
stored as the ground-truth symptom location.

Using a Gaussian splat rather than raw imagery gives the annotator a
coherent, navigable 3D model in which a symptom can be inspected from many
angles and localized precisely in the same metric frame as the robot's
map, which is what makes centroid-level 3D ground truth possible for real
trees at all. Figure~\ref{figSplatAnnotation} shows an orbit around an
annotated field splat, together with the detections that the semantic
planner accumulated on the same tree; the annotated regions and the
detected symptoms visibly coincide.

The most striking field result is how early the semantic planner separates
from the baseline. At viewpoint $15$---roughly half the budget, and thus
roughly half of the 3--5 minute inspection time---the semantic planner
already more than doubles the baseline's mAP ($0.209$ versus $0.089$) and
nearly quadruples its max-F1 ($0.461$ versus $0.124$). This early separation
is sharper in the field than in simulation, indicating that targeted
refinement of low-confidence regions is especially valuable under real-world
uncertainty, where a symptom is more likely to be missed or mislabeled from
any single view and therefore benefits most from deliberate re-observation.
By viewpoint $30$ the baseline's mAP catches up to the semantic planner's
($0.331$ versus $0.322$, a difference within evaluation noise), but only
after spending the full budget; the semantic planner reaches comparable
detection quality in roughly half the viewpoints, and thus roughly half the
inspection time, which is the operationally relevant advantage for a system
that must scale to many trees.

\subsection{Qualitative Observations} \label{secFieldQualitative}

% TODO: expand with concrete field observations once compiled from the

Beyond the aggregate metrics, several qualitative observations from the
deployment are worth recording. The flash-illuminated sensing behaved as
intended under variable outdoor lighting, suppressing the cast shadows and
specular highlights that degrade ambient-light imaging of bark
(Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}), which is the most likely reason the detector
generalized across the domain gap without site-specific retraining. The
consistency of detection performance between the training orchard and the
evaluation site, despite no overlap in imagery, supports the conclusion that
the learned symptom representations and the flash-illuminated pipeline
transfer to new orchards rather than overfitting to a single site.
The bottom row of Figure~\ref{figSplatAnnotation} gives a visual sense of
the resulting map quality: the detections the semantic planner accumulated
on tree 9 fall on the same regions of the canopy as the independently
annotated ground truth.

The main failure mode encountered in the field involved the arm's first
motions at a new tree. Before the map has been populated, the motion
planner knows nothing of the branches directly in front of the platform,
and in early runs the arm repeatedly brushed against near branches on its
way to its first selected viewpoints. The fix was the four mandatory
initialization viewpoints described in Section~\ref{secFieldProtocol}:
executing a short fixed sequence first populates the OctoMap with the
closest branches, after which the collision-aware motion planner routes
around them, and no further collisions occurred. This initialization was
adopted for every planner, and the simulation experiments were
re-evaluated under the same protocol so that the comparison between
settings remains matched.

\section{Discussion} \label{secFieldDiscussion}

The field experiments confirm the central simulation finding under real
conditions: adaptive, map-driven viewpoint selection substantially
outperforms deterministic coverage, and the disease-aware semantic planner
reaches high-quality detection maps markedly faster than either the baseline
or the volumetric planner. The division of labor between the two adaptive
planners also transfers: the volumetric planner remains the strongest at
geometric coverage, and the semantic planner the strongest at detection, in
the field as in simulation. The clearest difference between the two settings
is the magnitude of the semantic planner's early advantage over the
baseline, which is larger in the field. We attribute this to the higher
per-view uncertainty of real imagery: when any single observation is
noisier, the value of deliberately revisiting ambiguous regions---precisely
what the semantic information gain of Equation~\ref{eqSemIG} rewards---rises
relative to a fixed scan that observes each region only once.

Several practical bottlenecks bound the current system and frame the future
work of Chapter~\ref{chapConclusions}. The base is stationary during
inspection, so the system currently maps one tree at a time and depends on
an operator to reposition it along a row; autonomous row traversal is the
most direct path to orchard-scale deployment. Field evaluation was also
limited to 12 trees within a single two-day visit, constrained both by the
need to run all three planners on each tree and by the scarcity of standing
fire blight; broader evaluation across more orchards and seasons is needed
to characterize generalization fully. Detection quality, finally, remains
the dominant limit on absolute map accuracy: the planners can only refine
and localize the symptoms the 2D detector is able to recognize, so
improvements to the detector---including exploiting the multispectral
modality carried but unused here---would raise the ceiling on the whole
pipeline.

\section{Summary} \label{secErwinSummary}

This chapter integrated the components of the preceding chapters into Erwin,
a field-deployed mobile manipulation system for autonomous fire blight
inspection, built on an Amiga base and an xArm6 manipulator carrying the
multi-modal sensor rig, with all perception, mapping, and planning running
onboard under ROS 2 in a closed perception--action loop
(Sections~\ref{secRobotPlatform} and~\ref{secSystemArch}). Deployed on 12
dormant apple trees at the Penn State Fruit Research and Extension Center
under a genuine train--test domain gap, Erwin reproduced the simulation
findings of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}: both adaptive planners clearly
outperformed the deterministic baseline, the volumetric planner delivered
the most complete coverage, and the semantic planner delivered the best
detection quality, reaching comparable map accuracy to the fully-scanned
baseline in roughly half the viewpoints and half the inspection time
(Section~\ref{secFieldResults}). These results demonstrate that the
confidence-aware semantic map and semantic NBV planner are not merely
simulation artifacts but deliver fast, fine-grained disease maps under real
field conditions. Chapter~\ref{chapConclusions} draws together the
contributions of the full thesis, discusses the system's limitations, and
outlines the path toward orchard-scale autonomous disease mapping.
