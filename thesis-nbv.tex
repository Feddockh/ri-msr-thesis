\chapter{Semantic Mapping and Next-Best-View Planning} \label{chapNBV}

Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} developed a 2D detector that recognizes fire
blight cankers and shepherd's crooks in individual close-range images.
A single detection, however, is a per-view, per-pixel observation: it says
that a symptom is present somewhere in one image, but not where that
symptom sits in the three-dimensional canopy, nor how confident we should
be once the same region has been seen from several viewpoints.
The output a grower needs is the opposite of a single detection: a
persistent, spatially resolved map of disease confidence across the whole
tree, with a count of symptom instances per tree, that can be aggregated
into an orchard-wide severity map to guide targeted pruning and scouting
priorities.
This chapter bridges that gap in three steps.
First, Section~\ref{secSemanticOctoMap} introduces a confidence-aware
semantic occupancy representation that fuses noisy per-view detections into
a single volumetric map in which each voxel carries an occupancy
probability, a semantic disease label, and a fused confidence.
Second, because developing and evaluating a viewpoint planner on real
trees is slow and difficult to control (symptomatic trees are scarce,
ground truth is expensive to obtain, and no two field trees are
identical), Section~\ref{secSimulation} builds a simulation environment in
which symptomatic trees of known geometry can be generated and inspected
repeatably, and against which planners can be compared under identical
conditions.
Third, Section~\ref{secNBVPlanning} uses the semantic occupancy map as the
state for an active viewpoint-selection framework, formalizing the
next-best-view (NBV) problem and describing the three planners we
compare: a deterministic grid-scan baseline, a purely volumetric NBV
planner, and the semantic NBV planner that is the central contribution of
this chapter.
Section~\ref{secSimExperiments} then defines the evaluation methodology
and reports the simulation comparison of these planners.
The same representation, planners, and metrics are carried forward
unchanged to the field experiments of Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}.

\section{Semantic OctoMap Representation} \label{secSemanticOctoMap}

Geometric and semantic observations are fused into a single derived OctoMap
octree at resolution $r$, in which each voxel maintains a probabilistic
estimate of occupancy in log-odds form~\cite{hornung13octomap}, extended
with semantic attributes.
This unified representation is designed to support two distinct forms of
reasoning simultaneously: collision-aware reasoning about free and occupied
space, which the manipulator needs to plan safe motions through the canopy,
and task-aware reasoning about where disease is present and how certain
that evidence is, which the planner needs to decide where to look next.
Keeping both in one octree means that every candidate viewpoint can be 
scored against geometry and semantics with a single traversal of the 
same structure.

\subsection{Volumetric Occupancy with OctoMap} \label{secOctoMapBase}

We represent the geometry of the scene using an OctoMap, an octree-based
occupancy grid in which space is recursively subdivided into voxels down to
a minimum edge length $r$~\cite{hornung13octomap}.
Each voxel stores its occupancy as a log-odds value rather than a raw
probability, which makes repeated Bayesian updates from many observations
reduce to simple additions and makes the representation numerically stable
as evidence accumulates.
The octree structure is memory-efficient because large regions of uniformly
free or uniformly unknown space collapse to a single node, while detail is
preserved only where the scene is geometrically complex.

Given a sensor origin and a point cloud derived from the stereo depth
estimator of Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}, occupancy measurements are
integrated by ray casting.
For each measured point, a ray is traced from the camera origin to the
endpoint: voxels traversed along the ray are updated toward free space,
while the voxel at the endpoint is updated toward occupied space.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth, trim={0 232pt 0 232pt},
        clip]{figs/octomap_concept.jpg}
    \caption{Ray-casting occupancy update for a single sensor ray.
             A ray traced from the camera through the ROI to a
             measured surface point is converted by OctoMap into
             voxels updated toward free space along its path, a
             voxel updated toward occupied space at the surface it
             strikes, and voxels outside the ray's path left
             unknown (not visible).}
    \label{figRayCasting}
\end{figure}

This yields a compact volumetric reconstruction that preserves structure in
sparse regions while adaptively refining resolution in cluttered ones, and
that maintains three distinct occupancy states per voxel: free, occupied,
and unknown, the last being essential to the volumetric planner of
Section~\ref{secVolNBVPlanner}, which explicitly seeks out unknown space.

The map is updated incrementally as new sensor data arrives.
To bound both memory use and traversal depth, the OctoMap domain is
restricted to a $1.0\,\text{m} \times 1.0\,\text{m} \times 2.0\,\text{m}$
region loosely fitted to the target tree, and ray casting is terminated
early when a ray exits this region.
Constraining the spatial extent in this way keeps voxel read and write
operations fast enough to sustain the closed-loop inspection cycle, and
defines a consistent region of interest (ROI) against which coverage is
later measured (Section~\ref{secSimExperiments}).
Figure~\ref{figOctomapBuilding} shows this incremental filling-in process
across three viewpoints selected by the volumetric planner of
Section~\ref{secVolNBVPlanner}.

\subsection{Semantic Extension: Disease Class Labels}
\label{secSemanticExtension}

In addition to occupancy, each node in the semantic octree stores two
semantic attributes: a class identifier $c \in \mathbb{Z}$ (background,
canker, or shepherd's crook) and a scalar confidence $s \in [0,1]$.
Semantic attributes are integrated from the semantic point cloud described
in Section~\ref{secProjection}, in which every 3D point carries a predicted
class and confidence.
Crucially, semantic updates are applied only to voxels that have already
been marked occupied through geometric integration, so that disease labels
are only ever attached to physical surfaces and never to free or unknown
space.
Figure~\ref{figSemanticOctoMap} shows an example semantic OctoMap built
this way from a simulated symptomatic tree.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth, trim={0 100pt 0 100pt}, clip]{figs/semantic_octomap.jpg}
    \caption{A semantic OctoMap (left) built from a simulated
             symptomatic apple tree (right), with matched insets zooming
             into a single symptomatic branch in both the voxelized map
             and the underlying Blender mesh. Occupied voxels are gray
             by default and colored by fused disease class where a
             symptom has been detected: red for canker and green for
             shepherd's crook, matching the pair of nearby cankers and
             single shepherd's crook visible on the corresponding branch
             in the mesh inset.}
    \label{figSemanticOctoMap}
\end{figure}

The challenge in accumulating labels across views is that the 2D detector
is imperfect: the same patch of bark may be labeled canker from one
viewpoint and background from another, at differing confidences, and a
naive scheme that simply overwrites or averages these observations would
either discard useful evidence or wash out true detections.
We adopt the confidence-aware fusion rule of Burusa et
al.~\cite{Burusa2024SemanticsAwareNBVPlantParts}, extended here with a
per-match confidence boost $\gamma$ in addition to their mismatch penalty
$\lambda$.
Let $(c_t, s_t)$ denote the incoming class label and confidence for a voxel
at time $t$, and let $(\hat{c}, \hat{s})$ denote the voxel's current stored
estimate.
If no prior semantic information exists for the voxel, the incoming
observation is stored directly, $(\hat{c}, \hat{s}) \leftarrow (c_t, s_t)$.

If the incoming label agrees with the stored label ($c_t = \hat{c}$), the
two confidences are averaged and then boosted, rewarding repeated agreement
between independent views:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\hat{s}+s_t}{2}\,(1 + \gamma),\; 0,\; 1\right).
\label{eqSemMatch}
\end{equation}
The boost factor $(1+\gamma)$ causes a symptom that is consistently
observed across several viewpoints to accumulate high confidence, which is
exactly the behavior the semantic planner later exploits to recognize a
region as sufficiently explained.

If the incoming label disagrees with the stored label ($c_t \neq \hat{c}$),
the label carrying the higher confidence is retained:
\begin{equation}
(\hat{c}, \hat{s}) \leftarrow
\begin{cases}
(c_t, s_t) & \text{if } s_t > \hat{s},\\
(\hat{c}, \hat{s}) & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eqSemMismatch}
\end{equation}
and the retained confidence is then penalized to reduce overconfidence
under disagreement:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\hat{s}\,(1-\lambda),\; 0,\; 1\right).
\label{eqSemPenalty}
\end{equation}
The penalty $(1-\lambda)$ ensures that a voxel whose label is contested by
conflicting observations retains a low confidence, marking it as a region
that warrants further inspection rather than one the system can trust.
Together, the boost and penalty give the map a self-correcting character:
persistent, mutually consistent observations are reinforced toward high
confidence, while conflicting observations are driven toward low
confidence, allowing later high-confidence views to override earlier
detection errors.
Values for $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are given in
Table~\ref{tabPlanningParams}.

\subsection{Projection from 2D Detections to 3D Voxels}
\label{secProjection}

The link between the 2D detector of Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} and the
semantic octree is a per-view \emph{semantic point cloud}: a
depth-projected point cloud in which each point additionally carries a
class label and confidence.
Constructing it proceeds in two stages, rasterization and projection.

First, the instance segmentation masks produced by the detector are
rasterized into dense per-pixel class and confidence maps at the resolution
of the RGB image.
Every pixel is initialized to a background class with a fixed background
confidence.
Detected instances are then written into the maps in ascending order of
confidence, so that where instance masks overlap, the higher-confidence
detection takes precedence in the final per-pixel assignment.
This produces, for each captured frame, a class map and a confidence map
registered pixel-for-pixel with the RGB image.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth, trim={0 250pt 0 250pt}, clip]{figs/perception_pipeline.jpg}
    \caption{End-to-end perception pipeline from a single captured
             frame to the fused semantic map.
             \textit{Left:} the 2D detector output on the RGB frame,
             with the predicted class and confidence overlaid on the
             instance mask.
             \textit{Center:} the resulting semantic point cloud,
             back-projected from the detection using stereo depth
             and the camera pose, shown relative to the manipulator.
             \textit{Right:} the semantic OctoMap after this and
             prior views have been fused, with occupied voxels
             colored by disease class (Section~\ref{secSemanticOctoMap}).}
    \label{figProjectionPipeline}
\end{figure}

Second, each pixel with valid stereo depth is projected into 3D using
the camera intrinsics and the known camera pose, and the resulting point is
assigned the class and confidence stored at its pixel.
In simulation, this depth is taken directly from the simulator as
ground truth, rather than estimated by a stereo matcher.
Depth is clamped to the effective operating range of $0.1$--$0.9$\,m;
points outside this range are discarded as unreliable.
Pixels with invalid disparity or with depth beyond the maximum sensing
range may optionally be retained as background points placed at the maximum
range value, which allows the downstream occupancy update to carve out free
space and explicitly represent regions observed to be empty rather than
merely unobserved.
The resulting semantic point cloud is transformed into the common global
frame using the manipulator's forward kinematics and integrated into the
semantic OctoMap as described in Sections~\ref{secOctoMapBase}
and~\ref{secSemanticExtension}: geometry first, via occupancy ray casting,
then semantics, applied only to the voxels thereby marked occupied.
The full perception pipeline, from image capture through this projection
step, is described in its runtime, on-robot form in
Section~\ref{secPerceptionPipeline}.

\section{Simulation Environment} \label{secSimulation}

Real symptomatic trees are scarce, and runs in the field are costly,
which makes it difficult to use them for the rapid iteration and controlled 
comparison of different viewpoint planners.
We therefore built a simulation environment in which trees of known, fully
specified geometry can be inspected repeatably and under identical
conditions across all three planners, before committing to field
deployment.

\subsection{Orchard Simulation Setup} \label{secOrchardSim}

Simulated trees were modeled in Blender and inspected in a Gazebo
simulation of the robot, its manipulator, and its stereo camera
(Figure~\ref{figSimulation}).
Five symptomatic apple trees were generated, each carrying between five and
eight cankers and between five and eight shepherd's crooks distributed
across varying canopy geometries.
Symptoms were placed to reflect realistic outbreak patterns, deliberately
including instances that are occluded or awkward to view from a frontal
vantage point, so that the simulation would reward planners capable of
repositioning the sensor rather than only those that tile a frontal plane.
The Gazebo model reproduces the camera's field of view and the
manipulator's reachable workspace, so that the same viewpoint-feasibility
constraints that apply in the field also apply in simulation.

To increase canopy diversity without the cost of modeling additional trees,
each tree was augmented by rotating it about the vertical axis in
$60^\circ$ increments, yielding up to six distinct orientations per tree
and up to $30$ unique evaluation configurations across the five trees.
Because each rotation presents a different pattern of self-occlusion and a
different distribution of symptoms relative to the frontal viewing plane,
these augmentations exercise the planners on a wider range of
viewing challenges than five static trees would.

\subsection{Simulation Ground Truth} \label{secSimGT}

Quantitative evaluation of both mapping and planning requires a reference
set of true symptom locations against which the map's detections can be
scored, expressed as a set of 3D centroids, one per symptom, so that the
same evaluation procedure (Section~\ref{secSimExperiments}) applies
regardless of how the underlying ground truth was obtained.
For simulated trees, ground truth is exact: symptomatic instances are
segmented directly on the Blender mesh geometry, and the centroid of each
segmented instance is stored as the reference symptom location.

\section{Next-Best-View Planning} \label{secNBVPlanning}

With a representation that accumulates disease evidence in 3D, the
remaining question is where to acquire the next view.
We implement and compare three viewpoint-planning strategies for
unstructured orchard canopies: (i) a deterministic geometric baseline that
provides structured planar coverage without reasoning about the map, (ii) a
volumetric NBV planner that maximizes reduction of occupancy uncertainty,
and (iii) a semantic NBV planner that prioritizes regions of low semantic
confidence.
All three operate within the same closed perception--action loop and share
a common feasibility-filtering stage that guarantees every executed
viewpoint is kinematically reachable and collision-free.
The volumetric and semantic planners further share a single NBV selection
loop (Algorithm~\ref{algNBV}) and differ only in two components: their
candidate generator $\mathcal{G}(\cdot)$, which proposes where to consider
looking, and their information-gain function $\mathrm{IG}(\cdot)$, which
scores how valuable each candidate is expected to be.

\subsection{Problem Formulation} \label{secNBVFormulation}

We cast inspection as a sequential viewpoint-selection problem.
The state at each step is the current semantic OctoMap $\mathcal{M}$
together with the manipulator's current camera pose $x_t$.
The action space is a set of candidate viewpoints, each a full camera pose
(position and orientation), generated on demand from the current map by the
candidate generator $\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{M}, x_t)$.
Not every pose is executable, so candidates are intersected with the
feasible set $\mathcal{Q}$ of poses that are both within the manipulator's
reachable workspace and free of collisions
(Section~\ref{secWorkspaceFilter}).
Each feasible candidate $v$ is scored by a utility that trades its expected
information gain against the effort of moving there,
\begin{equation}
U(v) = \mathrm{IG}(v, \mathcal{M}) - \alpha\, C(x_t, v),
\qquad C(x_t, v) = \lVert x_t - v \rVert,
\label{eqUtility}
\end{equation}
where the motion cost $C(x_t, v)$ is the Euclidean distance from the
current camera pose to the candidate and $\alpha$ is a tunable weight
balancing exploration benefit against travel.
The planner selects the maximum-utility feasible candidate, commands the
manipulator to it, captures an image, and updates the map, repeating until
a fixed viewpoint budget is exhausted or the best remaining candidate's
information gain falls below a small threshold $\epsilon$, indicating
that no candidate view is expected to meaningfully change the map.
The three planners differ only in how $\mathcal{G}$ and $\mathrm{IG}$ are
defined; the baseline additionally forgoes the map-dependent scoring
entirely, as described below.

\subsubsection{Workspace Filtering} \label{secWorkspaceFilter}

Evaluating the kinematic feasibility of a candidate viewpoint through
inverse kinematics is far more expensive than scoring its information gain,
so testing every generated candidate this way would dominate planning time.
To avoid this, all candidates are first filtered against a precomputed
model of the manipulator's reachable workspace, following the approach of
Freeman and Kantor~\cite{Freeman2024AppleFruitletSizing}.
Offline, we densely sample on the order of $10^6$ end-effector
configurations and discretize the resulting reachable positions into a
fine-resolution voxel grid, in which each voxel stores a binary
reachability indicator.
At runtime, any candidate viewpoint whose position falls outside this
reachable volume is rejected immediately, before any motion planning is
attempted, and only the surviving candidates are passed to full feasibility
checking.

\subsection{Baseline: Grid Scan Planner} \label{secGridScan}

The baseline planner provides structured canopy coverage without reasoning
about map uncertainty at all, and stands in for the fixed-trajectory
scanning that a non-adaptive system, or a careful manual scout, would
perform.
A midplane is computed through the ROI, oriented orthogonal to the primary
viewing direction and positioned at the nominal inspection distance set by
the ROI bounding box.
A uniform grid of camera viewpoints is tiled across this midplane, with the
grid spacing chosen from the cross-section of the camera frustum at that
distance so that adjacent views overlap by a specified fraction $\rho$.
Concretely, for a camera with horizontal and vertical fields of view
$(\theta_h, \theta_v)$ inspecting at distance $d$, the planar footprint of
a single view is
\begin{equation}
w = 2 d \tan(\theta_h / 2), \qquad h = 2 d \tan(\theta_v / 2),
\label{eqFootprint}
\end{equation}
and the grid spacings are set to $\Delta_u = w\,(1 - \rho)$ and $\Delta_v =
h\,(1 - \rho)$ to enforce the desired overlap.
The viewpoints are then executed in a boustrophedon (row-alternating) order
to minimize manipulator travel between them.
This guarantees deterministic, repeatable planar coverage, but because the
grid is fixed in advance it cannot adapt to the specific architecture of a
tree or to what the map already contains: it will dwell on empty space and
skip past occluded symptoms indifferently.

Figure~\ref{figGridScanPattern} shows the resulting viewpoint grid and
execution order.

\subsection{Volumetric NBV Planner} \label{secVolNBVPlanner}

The volumetric planner selects camera poses that maximize the reduction of
occupancy uncertainty within the ROI, following standard information-gain
NBV formulations~\cite{Isler2016IGFormulation,
Delmerico2018VolumeticIGFormulation}.
It instantiates the shared selection loop of Algorithm~\ref{algNBV} with a
volumetric candidate generator and a volumetric information-gain metric.

\begin{algorithm}
\small
\caption[Next-Best-View selection]{Next-Best-View (NBV) Selection}
\label{algNBV}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Map $\mathcal{M}$, current pose $x_t$, feasible set $\mathcal{Q}$,
candidate generator $\mathcal{G}$, information gain $\mathrm{IG}(\cdot)$,
cost $C(\cdot)$
\Ensure Selected next-best view $v_{\text{next}}$
\State Update $\mathcal{M}$ with latest sensor data
\State $V_c \gets \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{M}, x_t)$ \Comment{Generate
candidate viewpoints}
\State $V_c \gets V_c \cap \mathcal{Q}$ \Comment{Filter infeasible poses}
\ForAll{$v_i \in V_c$}
\State $\mathrm{IG}_i \gets \mathrm{IG}(v_i, \mathcal{M})$
\Comment{Information gain from view}
    \State $C_i \gets C(x_t, v_i)$ \Comment{Motion cost}
    \State $U_i \gets \mathrm{IG}_i - \alpha C_i$ \Comment{Compute utility}
\EndFor
\State $v_{\text{next}} \gets \argmax_{v_i \in V_c} U_i$ \Comment{Select
best viewpoint}
\State Command robot to move to $v_{\text{next}}$
\State \Return $v_{\text{next}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Volumetric Viewpoint Generation} \label{secVolViewpointGen}

Candidates are drawn from two complementary sources.
The first is a midplane grid identical to the baseline's
(Section~\ref{secGridScan}), augmented with small spherical-cap orientation
perturbations so that each grid cell contributes several slightly rotated
viewpoints rather than a single fronto-parallel one; this preserves broad,
systematic coverage of the canopy.
The second is frontier-based: frontiers are defined as free or occupied
voxels adjacent to unknown space, and viewpoints are generated to look at
them directly.
To keep the candidate set tractable, frontier voxels are clustered using
$k$-means, with an upper limit of $100$ voxels per cluster, and for each
cluster centroid a set of viewpoints is sampled from a hemisphere facing
the centroid, at radial distances within a bounded range around the ideal
camera standoff.
The frontier source is what lets the volumetric planner actively seek out
and peer into unexplored pockets of the canopy, rather than only sweeping
the frontal plane.
The left branch of Figure~\ref{figSemanticVolumetricClustering} shows the
resulting frontier clusters and the candidate poses sampled around them.

\subsubsection{Volumetric Information Gain} \label{secVolIG}

For a candidate viewpoint $v$, volumetric information gain is estimated by
ray marching through the camera frustum and counting the unknown voxels
each ray would encounter, averaged over a set of rays $R$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}(v) = \frac{1}{|R|} \sum_{r \in R} U_r,
\qquad
U_r = \sum_{i=0}^{N_r} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{ \mathcal{O}(p_{r,i}) = \text{unknown} \right\},
\label{eqVolIG}
\end{equation}
where $p_{r,i}$ is the $i$-th sampled point along ray $r$, $N_r$ is the
number of samples along that ray, and $\mathcal{O}(\cdot) \in
\{\text{free}, \text{occupied}, \text{unknown}\}$ is the queried voxel's
occupancy state.
Each ray terminates at the first occupied voxel it reaches, when the
maximum sensor range is exceeded, or when the ray leaves the ROI, so that a
candidate is credited only with the unknown volume it could actually reveal
and not with space hidden behind a surface.
Figure~\ref{figVolIGRaymarch} shows this ray marching for one candidate
viewpoint; the resulting gain enters the shared utility of
Equation~\ref{eqUtility}, and feasible candidates are evaluated in
descending order of utility until a valid motion plan is found and
executed.

\subsection{Semantic NBV Planner} \label{secSemanticNBVPlanner}

The semantic NBV planner is the central methodological contribution of this
thesis.
Where the volumetric planner treats all uncertainty as equal (a voxel of
unknown space near a bare branch is as valuable to resolve as one near a
suspected canker), the semantic planner explicitly targets uncertainty
about disease.
It prioritizes viewpoints that would refine low-confidence disease
predictions over viewpoints that would merely reveal more empty space, on
the premise that, for a disease-mapping task, resolving whether an
ambiguous region is symptomatic is worth more than completing the geometric
reconstruction of an unambiguous one.
The NBV selection loop, feasibility filtering, and utility function
(Equation~\ref{eqUtility}) all remain unchanged from the volumetric
planner; only the candidate generator and the information-gain metric
differ.

\paragraph{Semantic Viewpoint Generation.}
Candidate generation mirrors the volumetric planner's two sources, with one
substitution.
The midplane grid with spherical-cap orientation perturbations is retained
unchanged, preserving broad canopy coverage.
The frontier-based source, however, is replaced: instead of clustering
geometric frontier voxels, we identify occupied voxels whose stored
semantic confidence is low (voxels the map currently regards as possibly
symptomatic but is not sure about) and cluster those with $k$-means,
sampling hemisphere-facing viewpoints around each cluster centroid exactly
as before.
The effect is that the planner's exploratory attention is drawn toward
contested, low-confidence disease evidence rather than toward geometric
unknowns.
Figure~\ref{figSemanticVolumetricClustering} contrasts the two candidate
generators on the same scene.

\paragraph{Semantic Information Gain.}
The semantic information gain augments the volumetric unknown-voxel term
with a semantic-uncertainty term, weighting the two by a mixing parameter
$\beta \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IG}_{\text{sem}}(v) = \frac{1}{|R|} \sum_{r \in R} \left( (1-\beta)\, U_r + \beta\, S_r \right),
\label{eqSemIG}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
S_r = N \sum_{i=0}^{N_r} \left( 1 - c(p_{r,i}) \right) \mathbf{1}\!\left\{ \mathcal{O}(p_{r,i}) = \text{occupied} \right\},
\label{eqSemGainRay}
\end{equation}
where $U_r$ is the unknown-voxel count from Equation~\ref{eqVolIG},
$c(p_{r,i}) \in [0,1]$ is the stored semantic confidence of the occupied
voxel at $p_{r,i}$, and $N$ scales the semantic term $S_r$ onto the same
numerical range as the volumetric count $U_r$ so that $\beta$ controls
their relative influence directly.
Each occupied voxel thus contributes in proportion to its \emph{lack} of
confidence $(1 - c)$: a confidently labeled surface contributes little,
while an ambiguous one contributes a great deal, which is what steers the
planner toward re-observing exactly those symptomatic regions the map is
least sure about.
As in the volumetric case, each ray terminates at the first occupied voxel.
Setting $\beta = 0$ recovers the purely volumetric planner, so $\beta$ can
be read as the degree to which view selection is disease-aware rather than
purely geometric.
Parameter values, including $\beta$, are reported in
Table~\ref{tabPlanningParams}.

\section{Simulation Experiments} \label{secSimExperiments}

We evaluate the three planners in the simulation environment of
Section~\ref{secSimulation}, comparing how quickly and how accurately each
builds a disease map as viewpoints accumulate.
This section first defines the evaluation methodology (how predicted and
ground-truth symptoms are matched, and how detection quality and geometric
coverage are scored) and then reports the results.
The methodology defined here is used unchanged for the field evaluation of
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup} \label{secSimSetup}

\paragraph{Predicted symptoms and matching.}
A predicted disease instance is represented as a cluster of connected
occupied voxels sharing the same semantic class, and its predicted location
is the mean 3D position (centroid) of the voxels in the cluster.
Ground truth symptoms are the 3D centroids obtained as described in
Section~\ref{secSimGT} for simulation and in Section~\ref{secGSSplat} of
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin} for the field.
A natural way to extend standard 2D detection evaluation, which matches
predictions to ground truth by bounding-box intersection-over-union
(IoU), to 3D would be to fit axis-aligned bounding boxes to each symptom;
however, fire blight symptoms stretch irregularly along branches with no
clear start or end, which makes axis-aligned boxes ill-defined and their
IoU unstable.
We therefore adopt a centroid-to-centroid formulation, matching a predicted
cluster to a ground-truth symptom when the Euclidean distance between their
centroids falls within a threshold $t$.
For orchard-scale scouting, branch-level localization at this granularity
is the operationally relevant target: a grower needs to know which branch
bears a symptom, not its exact surface extent.

Because voxel-cluster boundaries do not always align one-to-one with
individual symptoms, two corrections are applied to predicted clusters
before matching.
First, a cluster that spans multiple ground-truth points within the
matching threshold is split into sub-clusters, correcting for closely
spaced symptoms that voxel adjacency has merged into a single cluster.
Second, multiple nearby clusters that all fall within the threshold of the
same ground-truth point are merged, correcting for a single extended
symptom that has fragmented into several adjacent detections.
Together these corrections keep the evaluation from mistaking voxelization
artifacts at symptom boundaries for genuine missed or spurious detections.

\paragraph{Detection metrics.}
Detection quality is measured with two complementary metrics, both computed
on predictions and ground truth pooled across all trees at each viewpoint
index, rather than averaged per tree.
Pooling ensures that symptom-dense trees contribute in proportion to their
symptom count and avoids the undefined per-tree scores that arise on
healthy trees with no symptoms.

The first metric is the maximum F1 score.
Predictions are sorted by descending confidence and admitted incrementally;
at each implicit confidence threshold, the admitted predictions are matched
greedily to ground-truth points of the same class in ascending order of
distance, subject to the threshold $t$ and with the splitting and merging
above applied.
A matched prediction is a true positive, an unmatched prediction a false
positive, and an unmatched ground-truth point a false negative; F1 is the
harmonic mean of the resulting precision and recall, and we report the
maximum F1 attained over the confidence sweep.
The matching threshold is set to $t = 0.10$\,m, chosen empirically to
tolerate small centroid-localization noise while remaining tight enough to
separate neighboring symptoms.

The second metric is mean average precision (mAP), computed following the
COCO detection protocol with 3D Euclidean centroid distance replacing IoU
as the match criterion.
Predictions and ground truth are accumulated across all trees and matched
with the same procedure, applied independently at each of ten distance
thresholds from $0.02$\,m to $0.20$\,m in steps of $0.02$\,m.
The area under each per-class precision--recall curve gives that class's
average precision, mAP averages over all classes present in either the
predicted or ground-truth set, and over the ten thresholds.
Classes with predictions but no ground truth are included in the average
rather than excluded, so that false detections on symptom-free trees are
penalized rather than ignored.
Both metrics are recomputed at every viewpoint so that we can track how
detection quality evolves as views accumulate.

\paragraph{Coverage metric.}
To assess how thoroughly each planner explores the canopy geometrically, we
additionally report the coverage of the ROI, defined as the fraction of
voxels within the bounding box that have been assigned a known (occupied or
free) occupancy state:
\begin{equation}
\text{Coverage} = \frac{N_{\text{known}}}{N_{\text{total}}},
\label{eqCoverage}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{known}}$ is the number of voxels in a known state and
$N_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of voxels in the ROI.
Unlike mAP and max-F1, which are computed on the pooled cross-tree set,
coverage is averaged across trees at each viewpoint index, since each tree
has its own independent OctoMap bounding box.

\paragraph{Configurations and parameters.}
Simulation results are reported across the five Blender trees and their
$60^\circ$ rotational augmentations, for up to $30$ unique canopy
configurations in total (Section~\ref{secOrchardSim}).
Each tree is placed $1.0$\,m from the robot base to fix the sensing
geometry, and a $1.0 \times 1.0 \times 2.0$\,m bounding box is fitted to
each tree to define a consistent ROI across all planners.
Every run, for all three planners, begins with the same four fixed
initialization viewpoints, counted within the $30$-viewpoint budget;
this initialization was introduced for collision safety during the field
deployment (Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}) and is applied identically in
simulation so that the two settings remain comparable, which is why the
per-viewpoint curves of all planners coincide over the first few
viewpoints.
The detector deployed in simulation is the model fine-tuned on simulation
imagery (Section~\ref{secDetectionResults}).

\subsection{Results} \label{secSimResults}

Table~\ref{tabSimResults} reports planner performance at viewpoints $15$
and $30$, and Figure~\ref{figSimResults} shows the full per-viewpoint
trajectories of all three metrics.
The corresponding field results are reported separately in
Section~\ref{secFieldResults} of Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}, using the same
metrics and viewpoint checkpoints.
Two clear patterns emerge.
The volumetric planner achieves the most complete geometric exploration,
reaching the highest ROI coverage ($82.03\%$ at viewpoint $30$) by
aggressively driving down occupancy uncertainty.
The semantic planner, by contrast, achieves the strongest detection
performance, reaching the highest mAP ($0.479$) and the highest max-F1
($0.588$) by spending its later viewpoints refining low-confidence
predictions rather than exploring already-explained space.
The two adaptive planners track each other closely through most of the
sequence, with the semantic planner pulling ahead only in the later
viewpoints, once the volumetric planner's detection performance has
plateaued and there is little geometric unknown left for it to pursue.
The baseline planner trails on every metric at both checkpoints, confirming
that adaptive, map-driven viewpoint selection yields a meaningful
improvement over deterministic planar coverage in unstructured canopies,
and that this advantage holds whether the objective is geometric coverage
or disease detection.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis} \label{secSimQualitative}

The quantitative gap between the volumetric and semantic planners has an
intuitive geometric explanation, visible when their maps are compared at
matched viewpoint budgets.
The volumetric planner spreads its attention evenly over all unexplored
space, so its map fills in uniformly and its coverage rises fastest, but it
has no reason to linger on a branch whose disease label is uncertain once
that branch has been observed even once.
The semantic planner instead returns to exactly those ambiguous regions,
accumulating the repeated, mutually consistent observations that the
confidence-aware fusion rule of Section~\ref{secSemanticExtension} rewards,
and so resolves contested detections that the volumetric planner leaves
half-explained.
The result is that, at the same viewpoint budget, the semantic planner's
map contains fewer low-confidence symptomatic voxels and more confidently
resolved symptoms, at the cost of some geometric coverage in disease-free
parts of the canopy that it deliberately declines to explore.

Figure~\ref{figSimQualitative} makes this difference concrete on one of the
simulated trees.
Because the volumetric planner scores candidates by the unknown space they
would reveal, it distributes its early views over the whole ROI, including
the empty volume surrounding the canopy, and its map of the tree itself
accumulates comparatively slowly.
The semantic planner, drawn toward the voxels carrying uncertain disease
labels, concentrates its views on the tree from the outset and recovers
symptoms in the interior of the canopy several viewpoints earlier, while
the baseline, constrained to a frontal sweep, reaches the interior latest of
the three.

\section{Summary} \label{secNBVSummary}

This chapter developed the semantic mapping and planning core of the
thesis.
We introduced a confidence-aware semantic OctoMap that fuses per-view
detections from the Chapter~\ref{chapDataset} detector into a single
volumetric map, storing occupancy, a disease class label, and a fused
confidence per voxel, and updating semantics with a boost-and-penalty
fusion rule that reinforces consistent observations while flagging
contested ones for re-inspection (Section~\ref{secSemanticOctoMap}).
On top of this representation, we formulated inspection as a next-best-view
problem and described three planners: a deterministic grid-scan baseline,
a volumetric NBV planner, and the proposed semantic NBV planner, which
scores candidate views by the disease-label uncertainty they would resolve
rather than by geometric unknown alone (Section~\ref{secNBVPlanning}).
Using a Blender-and-Gazebo simulation with exact mesh ground truth
(Section~\ref{secSimulation}) and a centroid-based 3D evaluation protocol
(Section~\ref{secSimExperiments}), we showed that both adaptive planners
substantially outperform the baseline, that the volumetric planner attains
the most complete geometric coverage, and that the semantic planner attains
the best detection accuracy, reaching the highest mAP and max-F1 by
refining low-confidence regions in its later viewpoints.
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin} deploys this representation, these planners, and
this evaluation protocol unchanged on the physical robot, and tests whether
the simulation findings hold under real field conditions.
