\chapter{Data Collection and Disease Sensing} \label{chapDataset}

Realizing the active perception approach described in
Chapter~\ref{chapIntroduction} first requires a perception system
that can recognize fire blight symptoms in close-range,
dormant-season imagery.
This chapter develops that system from the ground up: the handheld
multi-modal camera rig used to collect orchard imagery, the dataset
assembled from multiple collection sessions across several rig
revisions, the SAM-assisted~\cite{ravi2024sam2} and cross-modal annotation
pipeline used to label that data efficiently, and the 2D instance
segmentation models trained on the resulting dataset.
The detector trained here is deployed as the perception front-end
for the semantic mapping and next-best-view planning system
presented in Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}.

\section{Sensing Platform} \label{secSensingPlatform}

Data collection was performed with a handheld, multi-modal camera
rig rather than the autonomous manipulator platform used for
inspection (Erwin), since rig development and dataset collection
began before the manipulator-based system was operational and
continued in parallel with it.
The rig combines a flash-illuminated stereo RGB camera, a 25-band
NIR multispectral camera, and, in early versions, a wide-angle
stereo RGB camera, all rigidly mounted to a common frame and faced
in the same direction so that a single operator could walk the
orchard rows and capture closely registered, multi-modal imagery
of trunks and branches at close range.
The rig went through several physical and electrical revisions
over the course of data collection, described in
Section~\ref{secDesignIterations}; unless otherwise noted, the
descriptions below refer to the final version used for the
majority of dataset collection.

\subsection{Flash Stereo RGB Camera (FLIR Firefly)}
\label{secFlirCamera}

The primary RGB sensor is a flash-illuminated stereo camera built
using FLIR Firefly cameras, developed by Silwal
et al.~\cite{Silwal2021FlashCamera} to provide consistent,
shadow-free illumination regardless of external lighting conditions.
This is especially important for dormant-season fire blight
inspection: cankers and shepherd's crooks are distinguished from
healthy bark largely by subtle texture and color cues (sunken or
darkened lesions, cracked bark) that are easily washed out or
obscured by the variable natural lighting
found in orchard rows.
The camera receives a hardware trigger command that
synchronizes the flash pulse with the stereo image exposure, so
that both the left and right frames are captured under identical,
controlled illumination.
The benefit is evident in the leftmost panel of
Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison}: the flash suppresses the cast
shadows that clutter the ambient-light iPhone image on the right,
and the high image quality brings out the contrast between the
raised, cracked canker tissue and the surrounding healthy bark.

Each Firefly camera has a resolution of 1440$\times$1080 pixels
with a calibrated focal length of approximately 1755 pixels,
corresponding to a field of view of roughly 44.6$^\circ$
horizontal by 34.2$^\circ$ vertical.
The stereo baseline, derived from the rectified projection matrix,
is approximately 60.4\,mm.
Depth is estimated using Foundation Stereo
\cite{wen2025foundationstereozeroshotstereomatching}, a learned
stereo matching method; because depth precision from a stereo pair
degrades as the square of depth
($\Delta Z \approx Z^2 / (f \cdot B)$), the 60.4\,mm baseline
yields a precision of approximately 2.4\,mm at 0.5\,m and
38\,mm at 2\,m.
For the semantic mapping and NBV experiments in
Chapters~\ref{chapNBV} and~\ref{chapErwin}, depth estimates from
the stereo matcher are clamped to the range 0.1--0.9\,m when
assembling the OctoMap, which constitutes the effective operating
depth range for that portion of the system.
The rig supports a maximum capture rate of 20\,fps, achievable
through software optimizations described in
Section~\ref{secStereoDepth} of Chapter~\ref{chapErwin};
in practice, both handheld data collection and robot-mounted
inspection captured a single triggered image at each position
rather than a continuous stream.

\subsection{25-Band NIR Multispectral Camera (Ximea)}
\label{secXimeaCamera}

A Ximea multispectral camera captures 25 narrowband channels in
the near-infrared range, using a snapshot mosaic architecture in
which 25 spectral filters are arranged in a repeating
5$\times$5 pixel tile across the sensor.
Since each tile contains all 25 filters, every band is captured
simultaneously in a single exposure, rather than requiring
sequential scanning or multiple shots.
This yields spectral information not visible in RGB, at the cost
of spatial resolution: each output band is effectively a
sub-sampled image, one pixel per tile.
The second panel of Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison} shows a
single NIR band of the same canker: the image is sharp and, like
the flash camera, largely free of the cast shadows that affect
ambient RGB, while the canker reflects the near-infrared band
markedly differently from the surrounding healthy tissue.

The Ximea's 675--951\,nm spectral range was chosen because
near-infrared reflectance has been shown to be discriminative for
disease detection, as tissue moisture content and pigmentation shift
with disease presence.
Skoneczny et al.\ found reflectance discrepancies of up to 10\%
between healthy and fire-blight-infected apple leaves across the
red-edge and near-infrared region (700--850\,nm), while Kurihara
and Yamana showed that red-edge band pairs spanning 660--760\,nm
achieve Valsa canker detection accuracy of 0.94 or greater on
apple tree bark~\cite{Skoneczny2020DetectionFireBlightAppleHyperspectralLeaf,
Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
We expect the red-edge region to carry diagnostic value for our
task as well, though the precise spectral patterns may differ:
both prior studies were conducted during the bloom season
(April--May), whereas our data were collected during late
dormancy (February--March); Skoneczny et al.\ studied fire blight
symptoms on leaves, which are absent during dormancy; and
Kurihara and Yamana targeted Valsa canker, caused by
\textit{Valsa mali} rather than \textit{Erwinia amylovora}
as in fire blight cankers.

The sensor used in this work has a resolution of
2048$\times$1088 pixels with a calibrated focal length of
approximately 2937 pixels, corresponding to a field of view of
roughly 38.4$^\circ$ horizontal by 21.0$^\circ$
vertical, a narrower, more telephoto view than the Firefly
stereo pair, concentrating spatial resolution on a smaller patch
of bark at close range.
After demosaicing, the effective spatial resolution per band is
409$\times$216 pixels.
The 25 bands span 675--951\,nm and are arranged in a 5$\times$5
mosaic tile that repeats periodically across the entire sensor
array, so that every 5$\times$5 block of raw pixels contains one
sample of each band; Table~\ref{tabXimeaBands} lists the center
wavelength at each position within this repeating tile.
Six of the 25 bands (675, 690, 715, 730, 743, and
757\,nm) fall within the 660--760\,nm red-edge window that the prior work
above identified as diagnostically relevant.

In early rig versions the Ximea camera was triggered
independently by software, while later versions placed it on the
same hardware trigger line as the flash stereo camera
(Section~\ref{secDesignIterations}), so that NIR and RGB frames
could be captured with tight, deterministic timing rather than
being subject to software scheduling jitter.

\subsection{Wide-Angle Stereo RGB Camera (ZED2i)} \label{secZED2i}

Early rig versions also included a Stereolabs ZED2i stereo RGB
camera, intended to provide a wider-field RGB overview of the
inspection area alongside depth for context and mapping.
Each eye captures 2208$\times$1242 pixels (2K) with a calibrated
focal length of approximately 1054 pixels, corresponding to a
field of view of roughly 92.7$^\circ$ horizontal by
61.0$^\circ$ vertical, substantially wider, at correspondingly
coarser angular resolution, than either the Firefly stereo pair or
the Ximea.
At this 2K resolution the camera runs at up to 15\,fps; like the
other sensors it was captured on command rather than streamed
continuously, but it supported only software triggering and so
incurred some timing slop relative to the hardware-triggered flash
stereo camera.
The effect of this wide, low-resolution geometry is apparent in
the third panel of Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison}: cropping
the wide-angle frame to the same canker yields a soft,
low-detail image in which the fine bark texture is lost.
For this reason the ZED2i was ultimately removed from later rig
versions, since it was not able to resolve the small-scale
features needed to detect dormant fire blight symptoms,
particularly bark texture on cankers and thin, small
shepherd's crooks, and contributed little to the dataset beyond
redundant coarse RGB context already covered by the flash stereo
camera.
Its wide field of view nonetheless remains well suited to
coarse tasks such as obstacle avoidance, for which the fine detail
required for symptom detection is unnecessary.

\subsection{Platform and Mounting} \label{secPlatformMounting}

The three cameras are stacked vertically on the rig frame, all
facing the same direction: the flash stereo camera at the top,
the Ximea multispectral camera centered beneath it, and, in early
versions, the ZED2i at the bottom.
A handle is mounted to the back of the rig for handheld operation
in the field, alongside a touchscreen used to interface with the
onboard computer.
In the field, the rig was driven by an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin
64\,GB module, which delivers up to 275 TOPS of inference
throughput within a 60\,W power envelope, powered by a drone
battery pack for untethered operation away from line power.
The Jetson and battery were housed together in an enclosure
carried in a backpack, with a cable bundle running from the
enclosure to the handheld rig, so that a single operator could
walk the orchard rows freely and trigger capture at each tree by
hand (Figure~\ref{figFieldCapture}).
The Jetson connects to all cameras over USB and records
synchronized data as ROS bags; incoming frames from each modality
are time-synced on capture so that downstream annotation and
label-transfer steps (Section~\ref{secCrossModal}) operate on
temporally aligned, registered imagery.

\subsection{Design Iterations and Synchronization}
\label{secDesignIterations}

The rig went through several design revisions driven primarily by
the practical demands of repeated field deployment and by the need
for tighter timing synchronization across modalities.

\textbf{Camera loading and mounting.}
In the earliest version of the rig, all cameras were loaded into
the base mounting block from the back, with the touchscreen then
attached behind them for viewing images and triggering capture.
Because the rig was frequently disassembled and reassembled in the
field, swapping cameras in and out of a back-loaded mount proved
cumbersome, so later versions switched to an entirely front-loaded
design, with slides added to lock each camera in place once
positioned.
The spacing between cameras was also reduced in later versions to
increase the overlap between their fields of view, improving the
consistency of cross-modal label transfer between the flash RGB
and NIR frames.

The flash stereo camera was always driven by a hardware trigger,
since the serial trigger command was needed to synchronize the
flash pulse with stereo exposure.
The Ximea and ZED2i cameras, in contrast, were originally
triggered by software, which introduced timing slop between
frames of different modalities.

\subsection{Camera Calibration} \label{secCameraCalib}

Cross-modal label transfer (Section~\ref{secCrossModal}) requires each
camera's intrinsics and the rigid extrinsic transform between the flash
stereo and Ximea cameras. Both were calibrated using the ROS 2 camera
calibration tool with a ChArUco board, whose per-square ArUco markers
allow the board pose to be recovered even when the board is partially
occluded or only partially in view (Figure~\ref{figCameraCalib}).
Calibration was repeated several times for each camera and accepted only
once the reprojection error fell below $1.0$ pixel, and the same target
was used to recover the rigid transform between the flash stereo and
Ximea cameras.

The Ximea additionally requires radiometric calibration: using the
in-field dark-calibration and flat-field correction (FFC) controls on
the rig (Section~\ref{secDesignIterations}), a flat frame is captured
against a uniform diffuse-white reflectance panel and a dark frame is
captured with the lens capped, and each raw pixel $R$ is corrected to
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{corrected}} =
    (R - D)\,\frac{\overline{F} - \overline{D}}{F - D},
    \label{eqFFC}
\end{equation}
where $D$ and $F$ are the per-pixel dark and flat responses and
$\overline{D}$, $\overline{F}$ their means, removing fixed-pattern
sensor and illumination nonuniformity before demosaicing.

\section{Data Collection Protocol} \label{secDataCollection}

Imagery was collected across three orchards, shown in
Figure~\ref{figFieldComparison}.
The first two are the primary image-collection sites, together
making up the multi-modal image dataset, while the third, at Penn
State, was a separate, previously unseen site reserved for
end-to-end testing of the full inspection system in
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}.
Of the two primary sites, the Pittsburgh orchard supplied the
annotated imagery used for both training and validation of the 2D
detector developed in this chapter; the Virginia orchard, though
collected and included in the dataset, presented only mild disease
with few symptoms and was ultimately left unannotated, so it does
not contribute to detector training or evaluation.
The two primary sites were selected to span a wide range of disease
severity, orchard architecture, and lighting conditions; their
principal attributes are summarized in
Table~\ref{tabCollectionSites}.

The first primary site, referred to throughout as the Pittsburgh
orchard, was a commercial orchard in western Pennsylvania, just
outside Pittsburgh, which we do not identify by name.
Data was collected there on February 12, 2025, from two rows of
symptomatic apple trees.
The trees were young, grafted onto a dwarfing rootstock and
trained using a trellis system, an architecture common in modern
high-density plantings.
Because the block was a working commercial planting rather than a
research site, its infection arose naturally rather than through
deliberate inoculation, and disease pressure was severe: cankers
and shepherd's crooks were both abundant.
Skies were overcast that day, giving diffuse, even illumination.
In total, 4{,}913 synchronized five-camera image sets were captured
at this site, amounting to 183.7\,GB of raw rosbag data.

The second primary site was the Alson H. Smith Jr. Agricultural
Research and Extension Center operated by Virginia Tech in Frederick
County, Virginia.
Data was collected there on February 14, 2025, from two rows of a
single block.
In contrast to the Pittsburgh site, this was an older, standard
orchard of self-supporting trees grown with minimal training and
wide spacing between trees.
The block had been deliberately inoculated, but disease pressure was
mild: a handful of shepherd's crooks were present and cankers were rare.
Skies were clear that day, producing strong, hard-edged shadows
across the canopy, the opposite lighting regime to the overcast
Pittsburgh session.
In total, 1{,}947 five-camera image sets were captured at this site,
amounting to 71.9\,GB of raw rosbag data.
Because symptoms here were sparse, this imagery proved less useful
for annotation and was ultimately left unlabeled; it remains part
of the collected dataset but does not contribute to detector
training or evaluation.

At both primary sites, the rows were imaged by walking
the center aisle and alternating between the left and right canopy
walls as symptoms were located.
The rig was held at a target standoff of 0.4--0.6\,m from the
canopy, consistent with the effective operating depth range of the
flash stereo camera (Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}), and viewpoints
were deliberately chosen to approximate those reachable by a
wrist-mounted camera on the manipulator, so that the collected
imagery would be representative of what the robot would later
acquire during autonomous inspection.
Both sessions were conducted between roughly 10:00 and 14:00 local
time.

The third orchard, at the Penn State Fruit Research and Extension
Center in Adams County, Pennsylvania, was held out entirely from the
detector dataset and used only to test the complete inspection
system in Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}, with field tests conducted from
March 18 to March 20, 2026.
The trees here were widely spaced but grafted onto a dwarfing
rootstock, and the block had been deliberately inoculated.
Disease pressure was moderate, between the severe Pittsburgh site
and the mild Virginia site: several cankers and a few shepherd's
crooks were present. Each next-best-view planner variant was
evaluated on the same set of 12 trees: 10 infected and 2 healthy.
In total, 3{,}299 synchronized image sets (without ZED) were captured
across the test site, amounting to 219.2\,GB of raw rosbag data;
because these rosbags also record lidar, GPS, and front-camera
streams alongside the inspection cameras, this site's total raw data
volume exceeds that of the Pittsburgh site despite its fewer image
sets.

\section{Dataset Description} \label{secDatasetDescription}

Note that the raw data volumes reported above are full rosbag sizes,
including all recorded sensor streams, rather than the size of the
extracted image sets; only a small subset of this raw data was
extracted into image sets and, of those, an even smaller subset was
ultimately annotated.
Of the 4{,}913 synchronized image sets captured at the Pittsburgh
site (Section~\ref{secDataCollection}), only 1{,}275 flash stereo RGB
frames were carried through to a labeled dataset.
This large reduction is a direct consequence of how difficult these
symptoms are to annotate: unlike the crisp, high-contrast objects of
general-purpose detection benchmarks, dormant fire blight symptoms are
subtle, irregular, and embedded in visually cluttered branch
structure, and each image demanded careful, deliberate annotation.
The remainder of this section explains why an existing dataset could
not simply be adopted for this task, and the following section
details the annotation protocol and pipeline built to label our own
imagery.

\subsection{Comparison to Existing Datasets} \label{secDatasetComparison}

No public dataset matches the conditions targeted in this work:
close-range, dormant-season imagery of cankers and shepherd's crooks
under flash and near-infrared illumination.
The large majority of fire blight imaging datasets are collected from
overhead unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) or aircraft, capturing whole
canopies from above to map disease incidence at the orchard scale
rather than to resolve individual symptoms on a
branch~\cite{Bagheri2020AerialDetectionFireBlightPear,
kang2020DroneDetectionFireBlightPear,
schoofs2020UAVFireBlightPearSpectral,
Xiao2022UAVFireBlightAppleMultispectral}.
This aerial vantage point is fundamentally mismatched to a
manipulator-mounted camera operating at arm's length from the canopy;
indeed, several of these studies note that dense foliage during bloom
and summer occludes symptoms beneath the tree crown, limiting what an
overhead view can
resolve~\cite{Bagheri2020AerialDetectionFireBlightPear,
Mahmud2023MaskRCNNFireBlight}.

The closest ground-level dataset is the ERWIAM dataset of
Ma{\ss} et al.~\cite{Mass2024ErwiamDataset}, to date the most
substantial annotated fire blight image collection, with 1{,}611
annotated RGB images captured across several consumer cameras and
smartphones.
Its four annotation classes (\texttt{flower}, \texttt{leaf},
\texttt{shoot}, and \texttt{maybe}, predominantly unhealthy
leaves) reflect a growing-season focus: three of the four target
tissue that is absent during dormancy, leaving only the
\texttt{shoot} class, which does capture shepherd's crooks, as
relevant to our setting.
A second relevant collection is the visible--near-infrared
hyperspectral dataset of Gaci
et al.~\cite{Gaci2023VisibleNIRDataset}; it primarily follows a small number
of lab-controlled plants over roughly two weeks to study infection
progression, and although it also includes a single image apiece from nine
infected field trees, that subset is sized for spectral analysis rather than
for the in-field symptom diversity needed to train a field detector.
Other efforts have collected field imagery closer to our setting,
namely the dormant-season canker images of Linker and
Dafny-Yalin~\cite{Linker2024MappingFireBlightCankersFRCNN} and the
apple fire blight imagery of Mahmud
et al.~\cite{Mahmud2023MaskRCNNFireBlight}, but neither is released
publicly with its annotations, so neither could be reused here.
Because we target dormant-season inspection and deliberately exploit
the flash RGB and NIR modalities (Section~\ref{secSensingPlatform}),
neither the imaging conditions nor the label taxonomy of these
datasets transfers to our task; therefore, building a dataset 
specifically tailored to our needs was unavoidable.

\subsection{Dataset Composition and Splits} \label{secDatasetSplits}

The annotated dataset comprises 1{,}275 flash stereo RGB images
(1440$\times$1080), all drawn from the Pittsburgh site, labeled for
the two dormant-season symptom classes developed in
Section~\ref{secManualAnnotation}: \emph{shepherd's crook} and
\emph{canker}.
Every RGB image was paired with a cross-modal NIR annotation
(Section~\ref{secCrossModal}), yielding a second, spatially registered
Ximea dataset with the same image membership, class definitions, and
train/validation/test partition; the ZED2i RGB stream was not
annotated and is excluded from the detection dataset entirely.

Splitting burst-captured field imagery naively by frame index or at
random is unsafe: consecutive frames may feature the same lesion 
from slightly different viewpoints as the operator moves past it, 
so a random split could
place images of the same lesion on both sides of the train/test
boundary and inflate the apparent performance of the detector. The
goal of the splitting procedure below is therefore to keep all
images of a given lesion within a single split.
To achieve this, every image was encoded as a 1280-dimensional
MobileNetV2 embedding~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, and the resulting
cosine similarity between consecutive frames was used, together with
the time gap between them, to partition the capture timeline into
contiguous chunks: the sequence was cut only where both a time gap of
at least 90\,s and a cosine similarity below 0.92 indicated a genuine
break in the walk, such as the operator moving to a new tree.
These chunks were then assigned to the
train, validation, and test splits by a randomized search that
balanced the canker-to-crook ratio across the three partitions, so
that each split sees a similar class mixture
(Table~\ref{tabClassBalance}).
Figure~\ref{figTemporalSplit} shows the resulting assignment along the
capture timeline: because chunks are assigned by class balance rather
than by contiguous blocks of time, the chunks belonging to each split
end up interspersed across the entire field walk, so every split
spans the full range of trees, viewpoints, and lighting encountered
during collection.

             (yellow), and test (red) splits along the capture
             timeline. The horizontal axis is time elapsed since the

    \label{figTemporalSplit}
\end{figure}

To confirm that this chunk-based split leaves no near-duplicate images
straddling a split boundary, we reused the same MobileNetV2 embeddings,
this time computing the cosine similarity for all image pairs rather
than just consecutive frames, and labeled each pair either
\emph{same-split} (both images in the same partition) or
\emph{cross-split} (images in different partitions).
Figure~\ref{figLeakage} shows the two resulting distributions.
As expected, the same-split distribution has a long tail of
high-similarity pairs, due to frames revisiting the same lesion that
correctly land together, extending past the 0.95 threshold
conventionally used to flag near-duplicate content.
The cross-split distribution, in contrast, falls off well before that
threshold: the single most similar cross-split pair reached only 0.916
for RGB and 0.903 for NIR, both comfortably below 0.95.
We manually inspected the highest-similarity cross-split pairs and
confirmed that each depicted a different lesion or vantage point rather
than a near-duplicate frame, corroborating the embedding-based result.
In plain terms, no two images that ended up in different splits are
anywhere close to being the same photograph, which is the evidence
that the split does not leak information from training into
validation or test splits.

\subsection{NDSI Band-Pair Separation Analysis} \label{secNDSI}

To identify the band pairs that best separate the two classes, we
follow the Normalized Difference Spectral Index (NDSI) analysis of
Kurihara and
Yamana~\cite{Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
For each pair of spectral bands $(\lambda_i, \lambda_j)$ with
$i < j$, the NDSI is computed from a reflectance spectrum $R$ as

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NDSI}(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{R(\lambda_j) - R(\lambda_i)}{R(\lambda_j) + R(\lambda_i)},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R(\lambda)$ is the reflectance at band $\lambda$.
Evaluating this on the per-image mean spectra defined in
Section~\ref{secClassSpectra} yields, for every band pair, a
distribution of NDSI values for the positive class and a separate
distribution for the negative class, each with one sample per
annotated image.
Assuming each class's NDSI distribution is approximately Gaussian,
it is summarized by a mean and standard deviation: $\mu_p, \sigma_p$
for the positive class and $\mu_n, \sigma_n$ for the negative class.
Two statistics are then derived for every band pair.

The first is a Mahalanobis-style \emph{separation}, which quantifies
class separability as

\begin{equation}
    D_M(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{|\mu_p - \mu_n|}{\sigma_p + \sigma_n}.
\end{equation}

\noindent This measures the gap between the class means in units of
pooled within-class standard deviation rather than as a raw
difference, normalizing for the fact that a given mean separation is
more meaningful when the class variance is low than when it is high.
Larger $D_M$ indicates a band pair at which the positive and
negative NDSI distributions are more cleanly separated.
The second is a \emph{classification threshold}, the NDSI cutoff at
which a one-dimensional classifier best separates the two classes,
taken as the point of equal Mahalanobis distance to both class means,

\begin{equation}
    T(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{\sigma_p \mu_n + \sigma_n \mu_p}{\sigma_p + \sigma_n}.
\end{equation}

\noindent This is a variance-weighted average of the two class
means, biased toward the class with tighter spread rather than a
simple midpoint.
Both statistics were computed across all $\binom{n}{2}$ band-pair
combinations of the $n$ available bands and visualized as heatmaps
indexed by $(\lambda_i, \lambda_j)$, following the presentation of
Kurihara and Yamana.
The separation heatmap identifies the band pairs carrying the most
discriminating power and is used to select the pair for
classification, while the threshold heatmap reports the specific
NDSI cutoff a classifier built on each pair would use.

Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
We attribute this shift to the differences in setting noted in

leaves and green bark, whereas our data capture dormant, woody bark,
where the dominant cue is a near-infrared reflectance-magnitude
offset between necrotic and healthy tissue rather than a red-edge
inflection.
The red-edge slope difference nonetheless still contributes, as
reflected in the threshold response for band pairs that span it.

\section{Annotation Pipeline} \label{secAnnotation}

Because no existing dataset fit the task, every label used in this
work was produced in-house.
We first attempted to bootstrap annotation with open-vocabulary,
text-to-annotation tools such as Grounding
DINO~\cite{liu2023groundingdino}, prompting them with descriptions of
cankers and shepherd's crooks, but these models failed to localize
dormant fire blight symptoms with any reliability. This is unsurprising,
since the subtle, texture-based lesions on bare winter bark bear little
resemblance to anything in their training distribution.
All symptom instances were therefore annotated by hand in
CVAT~\cite{cvat}, and the pipeline below was built to make that
manual effort as efficient as possible: a standardized labeling
protocol developed with a plant pathologist
(Section~\ref{secManualAnnotation}), SAM-assisted conversion of
boxes to masks (Section~\ref{secSAMAnnotation}), and geometric
transfer of labels from the RGB frames to the registered NIR frames
(Section~\ref{secCrossModal}).

\subsection{Expert Manual Annotation and the Symptom Taxonomy}
\label{secManualAnnotation}

The central difficulty in labeling this data is that there is no
established convention for annotating fire blight cankers and
shepherd's crooks with bounding boxes.
Shepherd's crooks are relatively uniform (all share the
characteristic hooked tip of a wilted, infected shoot), but cankers
vary widely in shape, location, and appearance, and their extent on
the bark is often ambiguous.
To make annotation repeatable across images and annotators, we
developed a written labeling protocol in consultation with plant
pathologist Srdjan A\'{c}imovi\'{c}, iterating on definitions and box
placement rules until each symptom type could be labeled
consistently.
The protocol first distinguishes one crook type and four canker
subtypes, summarized in Table~\ref{tabSymptomTypes} and illustrated in
the top row of Figure~\ref{figAnnotationDefs}.

annotated up to the nearest branching point, while keeping

\subsection{SAM-Assisted Annotation} \label{secSAMAnnotation}

The manual labels are bounding boxes, but the cross-modal transfer
step (Section~\ref{secCrossModal}) and the eventual move to instance
segmentation both benefit from pixel-level masks.
Rather than have annotators trace masks by hand, we convert each
labeled box to a mask with Segment Anything 2
(SAM2)~\cite{ravi2024sam2}, prompting the model with the annotated box
and having the annotator select the correct mask from its proposals.
This box-to-mask tool is used both to enrich the RGB labels and as an
intermediate representation in the label-transfer pipeline below; its
implementation is detailed in Appendix~\ref{apxSAM2}.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Label Transfer} \label{secCrossModal}

Annotating the NIR frames independently would have doubled an already
costly labeling effort, so instead we transfer the RGB labels onto the
registered Ximea frames geometrically, exploiting the fact that the
two cameras are rigidly mounted (Section~\ref{secPlatformMounting}) and
calibrated (Section~\ref{secCameraCalib}).
Section~\ref{secCameraCalib} details the ChArUco-based calibration
procedure and recovers the rigid transform between the flash stereo
camera and the Ximea (Figure~\ref{figCameraCalib}), as well as the
Ximea's radiometric flat-field correction (Equation~\ref{eqFFC}).

Given the calibrated cameras, labels are transferred in the four steps
shown in Figure~\ref{figCrossModal}.
Starting from a labeled bounding box on the flash RGB frame, SAM2
produces a mask of the symptom (Section~\ref{secSAMAnnotation}); the
per-pixel depth of that mask is read from the stereo disparity map,
computed with Foundation
Stereo~\cite{wen2025foundationstereozeroshotstereomatching} for its
high-quality reconstructions; the masked pixels are back-projected
into 3D using the RGB intrinsics and depth, then reprojected into the
Ximea frame using the calibrated extrinsics and NIR intrinsics.
The reprojected mask, and the box enclosing it, become the NIR
annotation.
To reduce residual error from calibration and depth noise, the
transferred label is optionally refined by running SAM2 once more on
the NIR frame, prompted with the reprojected box, so the final mask
snaps tightly to the symptom in the NIR image.
This pipeline gave usable NIR pre-annotations from the RGB labels at a
fraction of the effort of labeling the NIR frames from scratch; the
resulting NIR dataset mirrors the RGB dataset in image membership,
splits, and class balance (Section~\ref{secDatasetSplits}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figs/rgb-nir_annotation_pipeline.jpg}
    \caption{Cross-modal label transfer from the flash RGB frame to
             the registered Ximea NIR frame. (1) A symptom is labeled
             with a bounding box on the RGB frame; (2) SAM2 converts
             the box to a mask; (3) per-pixel depth is read from the
             Foundation Stereo disparity map and the masked pixels are

             into the NIR frame using the calibrated inter-camera

             SAM2 refinement on the NIR frame (not shown) optionally
             tightens the transferred mask.}
    \label{figCrossModal}
\end{figure}

To quantify how much manual effort this transfer pipeline actually
saves, we ran it unattended over all 1,275 RGB frames with existing
annotations and had a human annotator review and correct every
reprojected NIR box.
FoundationStereo and SAM2 were used out of the box in this pipeline,
with no fine-tuning or task-specific training on our data, so the
numbers below reflect a zero-shot starting point rather than a
ceiling.
Because the correction pass defines the ground truth, this evaluation
does not measure cross-modal detection accuracy against an
independent reference; it measures how close the pipeline's raw
output already is to a fully-corrected NIR label, i.e., the labeling
effort it removes.
Of the 1,275 frames, 44 (3.5\%) produced no usable prediction: in each
of these frames every annotated symptom reprojected outside the
Ximea's narrower field of view (Section~\ref{secXimeaCamera}), so
fewer than half of the reprojected mask points survived the
pipeline's validity filter, and the frame was excluded from the
statistics below and required fully manual annotation.
Other frames sometimes had an individual annotation fall outside the
NIR frame in the same way, but at least one annotation still
transferred successfully, so the frame was retained.

On the remaining 1,231 frames (1,632 ground-truth boxes, 1,788
predicted boxes), predicted and ground-truth boxes were matched
greedily by IoU and classified at each of three IoU thresholds into
four buckets: \emph{ok} (usable as is), \emph{adjust} (right symptom,
box needs resizing), \emph{add} (a missed symptom, drawn from
scratch), and \emph{delete} (a spurious prediction, removed).
Table~\ref{tabCrossModalEval} reports detection accuracy and the
resulting correction workload at IoU thresholds of 0.25, 0.5, and
0.75.
Class labels transfer perfectly since they are copied directly from
the source RGB annotation, so all residual error is spatial; results
are therefore reported class-agnostically.
At the practical IoU-0.5 threshold, 68.2\% of all annotations required
no correction at all, and only 4.3\% of ground-truth symptoms were missed
outright (\emph{add}). Adjusting an existing box's extent (294 cases) and
removing a spurious box (226) accounted for most of the residual effort,
while genuinely missed symptoms were rare (70): the remaining annotator
work is dominated by tightening and pruning boxes rather than by finding
symptoms the pipeline overlooked.

\section{2D Disease Detection Models} \label{sec2DDetection}

With the dataset assembled and labeled, we train the 2D detector that
serves as the perception front-end for the semantic mapping and NBV
system of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}.
Separate detectors are trained for the flash RGB and NIR modalities on
their respective registered datasets, following the identical splits
and class definitions established in Section~\ref{secDatasetSplits}.

\subsection{Model Architecture} \label{secModelArch}

Detection uses YOLO26-L, the large variant of the YOLO26
family~\cite{yolo26_ultralytics}, initialized from COCO-pretrained
weights and fine-tuned end-to-end (no frozen layers) for the two-class
problem of Section~\ref{secManualAnnotation}: shepherd's crook and
canker.
Earlier experiments used YOLOv8, a well-established baseline, but we
moved to YOLO26 for its faster training, improved accuracy, and a
segmentation head that provides a clean path from the bounding-box
detector used here to the instance-segmentation masks used downstream.
The same architecture is trained separately on each modality, and the
two detectors share the identical YOLO26-L graph with a single
exception: the stem convolution, whose input depth is 3 for the RGB
model and 25 for the NIR model, whose demosaiced Ximea bands feed the
first convolution directly.
Every layer downstream of the stem operates on the same 64-channel
feature map, so the two networks are identical from the first block
onward; widening the stem accounts for a difference of only 12{,}672
parameters out of roughly 26.2 million.
The consequential difference is therefore not the architecture but the
initialization: the RGB model fine-tunes the COCO-pretrained
\texttt{yolo26l.pt} checkpoint, whereas no 25-channel pretrained
checkpoint exists, so the NIR model is trained entirely from scratch,
learning its full feature hierarchy from the field data alone.
As Section~\ref{secDetectionResults} discusses, this asymmetry, rather
than the trivial parameter-count difference, is what separates the two
detectors' performance.

\subsection{Training Details} \label{secTraining}

Both detectors were trained for up to 200 epochs (early-stop patience
50) with the AdamW optimizer auto-selected by the framework, an
initial learning rate of $0.01$ with a fixed (non-cosine) schedule,
weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$, and a three-epoch warmup, on a single
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with automatic mixed precision.
A physical batch of 3 images was gradient-accumulated to a nominal
batch of 64.
The RGB model trained at 1088$\times$1440 with a mild augmentation
set: HSV color jitter, horizontal flips, small translation and scale
jitter, slight perspective, and mosaic (disabled for the final 30
epochs). These were empirically tuned, with weaker translate, scale, and
mosaic settings found to perform worse.
The NIR model is trained at 416 pixels, matched to the far smaller
demosaiced Ximea frames, and with all color-space augmentations
disabled, since HSV jitter is meaningless and numerically unsafe on
25-channel data; only the geometric augmentations (flips, translation,
scale) are retained.

modality-specific differences.

The RGB detector outperforms the NIR detector on both splits and under

Notably, the gap is wider at the stricter mAP@0.5:0.95 (0.300 versus

Because mAP@0.5:0.95 rewards precise localization, and precise
localization depends most on well-trained low-level features, this
pattern is consistent with the initialization asymmetry noted in
Section~\ref{secModelArch}: the RGB model inherits edge, texture, and
shape features from COCO pretraining, whereas the NIR model must learn
its entire feature hierarchy from scratch on the roughly 983 training
images, a dataset three orders of magnitude smaller than COCO.
The gap is also consistent with the NIR modality's lower native spatial
resolution: after demosaicing, each Ximea band is only
409$\times$216 pixels (Section~\ref{secXimeaCamera}), so the NIR
detector regresses box boundaries against coarser spatial detail
regardless of initialization.
The two models being otherwise architecturally identical, initialization
and native spatial resolution are the two most plausible explanations
for the performance difference.
Encouragingly, test performance closely tracks validation for both
models (mAP@0.5:0.95 is in fact marginally higher on the test split
than on validation), indicating that the leakage-free split of
Section~\ref{secDatasetSplits} yields an honest estimate of
generalization rather than a memorized one.
Peak $F_1$ is reached at a moderate confidence (roughly 0.19--0.30 across

suppress the resulting weak false positives, so that faint but genuine

\subsection{Failure Mode Analysis} \label{secFailureModes}

Beyond the raw accuracy numbers, a systematic failure mode of the
detector is worth examining, because it exposes a limitation of
standard detection metrics on this kind of data and motivates the
mapping-based approach of the following chapters.
The issue is that cankers and shepherd's crooks have no well-defined
spatial extent: a canker often fades gradually into healthy bark with no
crisp boundary, and the lower end of a shepherd's crook blends into
the stem below it.
Annotators must nonetheless commit to a specific box, and two
reasonable annotators, or the annotator and the detector, will
routinely draw boxes that cover the same lesion on the same branch but
differ in where they start and stop.
Under the standard mAP@0.5 matching rule, such a prediction is scored
as a miss whenever its intersection-over-union with the ground-truth
box falls below 0.5, even though it correctly identifies the diseased
tissue.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth, trim={200pt 0 0 0}, clip]{figs/map_05_03_fns.jpg}
    \caption{False negatives arising from ambiguous symptom
             boundaries. A prediction and its corresponding
             ground-truth box are colored red and yellow,
             respectively, when unmatched, and green and magenta,
             respectively, once matched. Top row: under the standard
             IoU~$\geq 0.5$ matching rule, the detector's predictions
             (red, with confidence) correctly land on diseased
             branches, but their bounding boxes do not overlap the
             ground-truth boxes (yellow) closely enough, so the ground
             truth is scored as a false negative (``FN'') and the
             prediction as a false positive. Bottom row: the same
             boxes become true positives (green predictions, magenta
             ground truth) once the matching threshold is relaxed to
             IoU~$\geq 0.3$.}
    \label{figFalseNegatives}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{figFalseNegatives} shows two representative cases.
In each, the detector's prediction lands squarely on a diseased branch
and would be judged correct by a human, yet because its box does not
overlap the ground-truth box closely enough, the ground truth is
counted as a false negative and the prediction as a false positive.
The bottom row of the figure confirms that these are near-misses
rather than genuine detection failures: the same boxes match once the
overlap requirement is loosened.
This near-miss pattern is not isolated to the two examples shown:
relaxing the matching threshold to IoU~$\geq 0.3$ on the test split
recovers substantially more matches for both models, as shown by the
jump in mAP and $F_1$ in Table~\ref{tabDetectionResults03} relative
to the IoU~$\geq 0.5$ figures of Table~\ref{tabDetectionResults}.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Detection performance of the RGB and NIR YOLO26-L models
             on the test split, comparing the conventional
             IoU~$\geq 0.5$ matching threshold (repeated from
             Table~\ref{tabDetectionResults}) to the relaxed
             IoU~$\geq 0.3$ threshold. Max $F_1$ is the peak of the
             class-averaged $F_1$-confidence curve.}
    \label{tabDetectionResults03}
    \begin{tabular}{l|cc|cc}
        \hline
        Model & mAP@0.5 & mAP@0.3 & $F_1$@0.5 & $F_1$@0.3 \\
        \hline
        RGB & 0.605 & 0.734 & 0.62 & 0.72 \\
        NIR & 0.565 & 0.703 & 0.56 & 0.65 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}

Relaxing the matching criterion from IoU~$\geq 0.5$ to IoU~$\geq 0.3$
raises both mAP and $F_1$ substantially for both models (RGB: 0.605 to
0.734 mAP, 0.62 to 0.72 $F_1$; NIR: 0.565 to 0.703 mAP, 0.56 to 0.65
$F_1$), indicating that a meaningful share of the apparent misses at
the conventional threshold are boxes that overlap the ground truth but
not tightly enough to count, rather than symptoms missed outright.
We show these relaxed-threshold results only to characterize how much
of the apparent failure rate is boundary ambiguity rather than missed
detections; we do not adopt IoU~$\geq 0.3$ as a general-purpose
replacement for the conventional IoU~$\geq 0.5$ matching criterion used
elsewhere in this thesis.
The broader lesson is that standard detection metrics can only say
so much about performance in the field on symptoms with ambiguous
boundaries; aggregating evidence across many views of the same tree,
as the semantic mapping and NBV planning of Chapter~\ref{chapNBV} do,
is a more robust way to convert imperfect per-frame detections into
reliable tree-level diagnoses.

\section{Summary} \label{secDatasetSummary}

This chapter built the perception system underlying the rest of the
thesis, from sensor to trained detector.
We described a handheld, multi-modal camera rig combining a
flash-illuminated stereo RGB camera and a 25-band NIR multispectral
camera, and the field collection protocol used to gather close-range,
dormant-season imagery across three orchards.
Because no existing dataset matched these conditions, we assembled our
own: 1{,}275 flash RGB images from the Pittsburgh site, each paired
with a registered NIR frame, labeled for two dormant-season symptom
classes, shepherd's crook and canker, under an annotation protocol
developed with a plant pathologist and partitioned into leakage-free
train, validation, and test splits.
To make hand-labeling this data tractable, we built an annotation
pipeline combining SAM2-assisted box-to-mask conversion with geometric
cross-modal label transfer from the RGB frames to the NIR frames.
A spectral analysis of the resulting NIR labels confirmed that the
red-edge and near-infrared region carries discriminative information
for dormant fire blight, and separate YOLO26 detectors were trained on
each modality; the RGB detector outperformed the NIR detector on both
mAP and $F_1$, so the RGB model was carried forward as the detector
used in the rest of the thesis.
Finally, a failure-mode analysis showed that the ambiguous boundaries
of these symptoms cause standard detection metrics to understate
real-world performance, motivating the multi-view semantic mapping and
next-best-view planning developed in Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}, where this
detector is deployed as the perception front-end.
