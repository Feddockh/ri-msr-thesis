\chapter{Data Collection and 2D Disease Detection} \label{chapDataset}

Realizing the active perception approach described in
Chapter~\ref{chapIntroduction} first requires a perception system
that can recognize fire blight symptoms in close-range,
dormant-season imagery.
This chapter develops that system from the ground up: the handheld
multi-modal camera rig used to collect orchard imagery, the dataset
assembled from multiple collection sessions across several rig
revisions, the SAM-assisted and cross-modal annotation pipeline
used to label that data efficiently, and the 2D instance
segmentation models trained on the resulting dataset.
The detector trained here is deployed as the perception front-end
for the semantic mapping and next-best-view planning system
presented in Chapter~\ref{chapNBV}.

\section{Sensing Platform} \label{secSensingPlatform}

Data collection was performed with a handheld, multi-modal camera
rig rather than the autonomous manipulator platform used for
inspection (Erwin), since rig development and dataset collection
began before the manipulator-based system was operational and
continued in parallel with it.
The rig combines a flash-illuminated stereo RGB camera, a 25-band
NIR multispectral camera, and, in early versions, a wide-angle
stero RGB camera, all rigidly mounted to a common frame and faced
in the same direction so that a single operator could walk the
orchard rows and capture closely registered, multi-modal imagery
of trunks and branches at close range.
The rig went through several physical and electrical revisions
over the course of data collection, described in
Section~\ref{secDesignIterations}; unless otherwise noted, the
descriptions below refer to the final version used for the
majority of dataset collection.

\subsection{Flash Stereo RGB Camera (FLIR Firefly)}
\label{secFlirCamera}

The primary RGB sensor is a flash-illuminated stereo camera built
around FLIR Firefly cameras, developed by Silwal
et al.~\cite{Silwal2021FlashCamera} to provide consistent,
shadow-free illumination regardless of ambient lighting conditions.
This is especially important for dormant-season fire blight
inspection: cankers and shepherd's crooks are distinguished from
healthy bark largely by subtle texture and color cues (sunken or
darkened lesions, cracked bark) that are easily washed out or
obscured by the variable, often low and oblique, natural lighting
found in orchard rows.
The camera receives a hardware (serial) trigger command that
synchronizes the flash pulse with the stereo image exposure, so
that both the left and right frames are captured under identical,
controlled illumination.
The benefit is evident in the leftmost panel of
Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison}: the flash suppresses the cast
shadows and specular highlights that clutter the ambient-light
iPhone image on the right, and the high image quality brings out
the contrast between the raised, cracked canker tissue and the
surrounding healthy bark.

Each Firefly camera has a resolution of 1440$\times$1080 pixels
with a calibrated focal length of approximately 1755 pixels,
corresponding to a field of view of roughly 44.6$^\circ$
horizontal by 34.2$^\circ$ vertical.
The stereo baseline, derived from the rectified projection matrix,
is approximately 60.4\,mm.
Depth is estimated using Foundation Stereo
\cite{wen2025foundationstereozeroshotstereomatching}, a learned
stereo matching method; because depth precision from a stereo pair
degrades as the square of depth
($\Delta Z \approx Z^2 / (f \cdot B)$), the 60.4\,mm baseline
yields a precision of approximately 2.4\,mm at 0.5\,m and
38\,mm at 2\,m.
For the semantic mapping and NBV experiments in
Chapters~\ref{chapNBV} and~\ref{chapErwin}, depth estimates from
the stereo matcher are clamped to the range 0.1--0.9\,m when
assembling the OctoMap, which constitutes the effective operating
depth range for that portion of the system.
The rig supports a maximum capture rate of 20\,fps, achievable
through software optimizations described in
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}; % TODO: give exact subsection ref once written
in practice, both handheld data collection and robot-mounted
inspection captured a single triggered image at each position
rather than a continuous stream.

\subsection{25-Band NIR Multispectral Camera (Ximea)}
\label{secXimeaCamera}

A Ximea multispectral camera captures 25 narrowband channels in
the near-infrared range, using a snapshot mosaic architecture in
which 25 spectral filters are arranged in a repeating
5$\times$5 pixel tile across the sensor.
Since each tile contains all 25 filters, every band is captured
simultaneously in a single exposure, rather than requiring
sequential scanning or multiple shots.
This yields spectral information not visible in RGB, at the cost
of spatial resolution: each output band is effectively a
sub-sampled image, one pixel per tile.
The second panel of Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison} shows a
single NIR band of the same canker: the image is sharp and, like
the flash camera, largely free of the cast shadows that affect
ambient RGB, while the canker reflects the near-infrared band
markedly differently from the surrounding healthy tissue.

This spectral range was chosen because near-infrared reflectance
has been shown to be discriminative for disease detection, as
tissue moisture content and pigmentation shift with disease presence.
Skoneczny et al.\ found reflectance discrepancies of up to 10\%
between healthy and fire-blight-infected apple leaves across the
red-edge and near-infrared region (700--850\,nm), while Kurihara
and Yamana showed that red-edge band pairs spanning 660--760\,nm
achieve Valsa canker detection accuracy of 0.94 or greater on
apple tree bark~\cite{Skoneczny2020DetectionFireBlightAppleHyperspectralLeaf,
Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
We expect the red-edge region to carry diagnostic value for our
task as well, though the precise spectral patterns may differ:
both prior studies were conducted during the bloom season
(April--May), whereas our data were collected during late
dormancy (February--March); Skoneczny et al.\ studied fire blight
symptoms on leaves, which are absent during dormancy; and
Kurihara and Yamana targeted Valsa canker, caused by
\textit{Valsa mali} rather than \textit{Erwinia amylovora}
as in fire blight cankers.

The sensor used in this work has a resolution of
2048$\times$1088 pixels with a calibrated focal length of
approximately 2937 pixels, corresponding to a field of view of
roughly 38.4$^\circ$ horizontal by 21.0$^\circ$
vertical---a narrower, more telephoto view than the Firefly
stereo pair, concentrating spatial resolution on a smaller patch
of bark at close range.
After demosaicing, the effective spatial resolution per band is
409$\times$216 pixels.
The 25 bands span 675--951\,nm, and Table~\ref{tabXimeaBands}
lists the center wavelength at each position in the mosaic tile;
six of the 25 bands---675, 690, 715, 730, 743, and
757\,nm---fall within the 660--760\,nm red-edge window identified
above as diagnostically relevant.

In early rig versions the Ximea camera was triggered
independently by software, while later versions placed it on the
same hardware trigger line as the flash stereo camera
(Section~\ref{secDesignIterations}), so that NIR and RGB frames
could be captured with tight, deterministic timing rather than
being subject to software scheduling jitter.

\subsection{Wide-Angle Stereo RGB Camera (ZED2i)} \label{secZED2i}

Early rig versions also included a Stereolabs ZED2i stereo RGB
camera, intended to provide a wider-field RGB overview of the
inspection area alongside depth for context and mapping.
Each eye captures 2208$\times$1242 pixels (2K) with a calibrated
focal length of approximately 1054 pixels, corresponding to a
field of view of roughly 92.7$^\circ$ horizontal by
61.0$^\circ$ vertical---substantially wider, at correspondingly
coarser angular resolution, than either the Firefly stereo pair or
the Ximea.
At this 2K resolution the camera runs at up to 15\,fps; like the
other sensors it was captured on command rather than streamed
continuously, but it supported only software triggering and so
incurred some timing slop relative to the hardware-triggered flash
stereo camera.
The effect of this wide, low-resolution geometry is apparent in
the third panel of Figure~\ref{figCameraComparison}: cropping
the wide-angle frame to the same canker yields a soft,
low-detail image in which the fine bark texture is lost.
For this reason the ZED2i was ultimately removed from later rig
versions, since it was not able to resolve the small-scale
features needed to detect dormant fire blight symptoms,
particularly bark texture on cankers and thin, small
shepherd's crooks, and contributed little to the dataset beyond
redundant coarse RGB context already covered by the flash stereo
camera.
Its wide field of view nonetheless remains well suited to
coarse tasks such as obstacle avoidance, for which the fine detail
required for symptom detection is unnecessary.

\subsection{Platform and Mounting} \label{secPlatformMounting}

The three cameras are stacked vertically on the rig frame, all
facing the same direction: the flash stereo camera at the top,
the Ximea multispectral camera centered beneath it, and, in early
versions, the ZED2i at the bottom.
A handle is mounted to the back of the rig for handheld operation
in the field, alongside a touchscreen used to interface with the
onboard computer.
In the field, the rig was driven by an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin
64\,GB module, which delivers up to 275 TOPS of inference
throughput within a 60\,W power envelope, powered by a drone
battery pack for untethered operation away from line power.
The Jetson and battery were housed together in an enclosure
carried in a backpack, with a cable bundle running from the
enclosure to the handheld rig, so that a single operator could
walk the orchard rows freely and trigger capture at each tree by
hand (Figure~\ref{figFieldCapture}).
The Jetson connects to all cameras over USB and records
synchronized data as ROS bags; incoming frames from each modality
are time-synced on capture so that downstream annotation and
label-transfer steps (Section~\ref{secCrossModal}) operate on
temporally aligned, registered imagery.

\subsection{Design Iterations and Triggering}
\label{secDesignIterations}

The rig went through several design revisions driven primarily by
the practical demands of repeated field deployment and by the need
for tighter timing synchronization across modalities.

\textbf{Camera loading and mounting.}
In the earliest version of the rig, all cameras were loaded into
the base mounting block from the back, with the touchscreen then
attached behind them for viewing images and triggering capture.
Because the rig was frequently disassembled and reassembled in the
field, swapping cameras in and out of a back-loaded mount proved
cumbersome, so later versions switched to an entirely front-loaded
design, with slides added to lock each camera in place once
positioned.
The spacing between cameras was also reduced in later versions to
increase the overlap between their fields of view, improving the
consistency of cross-modal label transfer between the flash RGB
and NIR frames.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth, trim={0 400pt 0 400pt}, clip]{figs/v1_v2_camera_rig.jpg}
    \caption{Evolution of the handheld camera rig across two design
             versions, each shown as a CAD model (left) and the
             assembled hardware (right).
             \textit{Left:} the initial back-loaded design, in which
             the cameras were inserted from behind and the
             touchscreen was bolted on behind them.
             \textit{Right:} the revised front-loaded design, in
             which sliding mounts lock each camera in place for
             quick swaps in the field and the inter-camera spacing
             is reduced to increase field-of-view overlap.}
    \label{figRigEvolution}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Triggering and synchronization.}
The flash stereo camera was always driven by a hardware trigger,
since the serial trigger command was needed to synchronize the
flash pulse with stereo exposure.
The Ximea and ZED2i cameras, in contrast, were originally
triggered by software, which introduced timing slop between
frames of different modalities.
% TODO: quantify typical software-trigger timing offset, if known.
In later rig versions, the Ximea camera was moved onto the same
hardware trigger line as the flash stereo camera, eliminating this
slop between the two modalities retained in the final design; the
ZED2i was dropped from the platform around the same time for the
resolution limitations described in Section~\ref{secZED2i}.

\textbf{Control interface.}
The control software was ported from Python to C++ to improve
capture and preview throughput, and the user interface was
progressively redesigned around large, glove-friendly controls
suited to operation on the small rig touchscreen outdoors; its
evolution across three versions is shown in
Figure~\ref{figGuiEvolution}.
The initial version presented four camera views alongside a single
small button that triggered one capture at a time.
When the Ximea camera was added, its NIR preview was included and
the redundant right image of a stereo pair was dropped so that the
remaining views could be shown larger and their content inspected
more easily.
The single-shot trigger was augmented with a sequence-capture mode,
the status box was shrunk and the buttons enlarged, and ROS bag
recording controls were added; after each save the status field
reported the resulting bag size, so the operator could confirm data
was being written while walking the rows.
A column of sliders was added in the middle of the interface to
adjust camera settings directly in the field---in the second
version these exposed the flash duration, Firefly (flash camera)
exposure, Ximea gain, and Ximea exposure---along with buttons to run
flat-field correction (FFC) calibration on the Ximea without leaving
the orchard.
In the final version the sliders were widened to dominate the middle
column, making them easier to drag on the small screen, and a
frame-rate (Hz) control was added; the single-shot trigger was
removed entirely, so the start button simply began repeating
captures at the selected rate.
By this point the ZED2i had been removed from the rig, leaving only
the flash RGB and Ximea NIR previews, the two modalities retained
for the final dataset, stacked on the right side of the interface.

\section{Data Collection Protocol} \label{secDataCollection}

Imagery was collected across three orchards, shown in
Figure~\ref{figFieldComparison}.
The first two are the primary image-collection sites, together
making up the multi-modal image dataset, while the third, at Penn
State, was a separate, previously unseen site reserved for
end-to-end testing of the full inspection system in
Chapter~\ref{chapErwin}.
Of the two primary sites, the Pittsburgh orchard supplied the
annotated imagery used for both training and validation of the 2D
detector developed in this chapter; the Virginia orchard, though
collected and included in the dataset, presented only mild disease
with few symptoms and was ultimately left unannotated, so it does
not contribute to detector training or evaluation.
The two primary sites were selected to span a wide range of disease
severity, orchard architecture, and lighting conditions; their
principal attributes are summarized in
Table~\ref{tabCollectionSites}.

The first primary site was a commercial orchard in western
Pennsylvania, just outside Pittsburgh, which we do not identify by
name.
Data were collected there on February 12, 2025, from two rows of
symptomatic apple trees.
The trees were young, grafted onto a high-density rootstock and
trained as a dwarf central leader, an architecture common in modern
high-density plantings.
Because the block was a working commercial planting rather than a
research site, its infection arose naturally rather than through
deliberate inoculation, and disease pressure was severe: cankers
and shepherd's crooks were both abundant.
Skies were overcast that day, giving diffuse, even illumination.
In total, 4{,}913 synchronized five-camera image sets were captured
at this site, amounting to 78.9\,GB of raw data.

The second primary site was the Alson H. Smith Jr. Agricultural
Research and Extension Center operated by Virginia Tech in Frederick
County, Virginia.
Data were collected there on February 14, 2025, from two rows of a
single block.
In contrast to the Pittsburgh site, this was an older, free-standing
orchard of self-supporting trees grown with minimal training and
wide spacing between trees.
The block had been deliberately inoculated, but disease pressure was
mild: a handful of shepherd's crooks were present and no cankers.
Skies were clear that day, producing strong, hard-edged shadows
across the canopy---the opposite lighting regime to the overcast
Pittsburgh session.
In total, 1{,}947 five-camera image sets were captured at this site,
amounting to 31.9\,GB of raw data.
Because symptoms here were sparse, this imagery proved less useful
for annotation and was ultimately left unlabeled; it remains part
of the collected dataset but does not contribute to detector
training or evaluation.

At both primary sites, the two rows of a pair were imaged by walking
the center aisle and alternating between the left and right canopy
walls as symptoms were located.
The rig was held at a target standoff of 0.4--0.6\,m from the
canopy, consistent with the effective operating depth range of the
flash stereo camera (Section~\ref{secFlirCamera}), and viewpoints
were deliberately chosen to approximate those reachable by a
wrist-mounted camera on the manipulator, so that the collected
imagery would be representative of what the robot would later
acquire during autonomous inspection.
Both sessions were conducted between roughly 10:00 and 16:00 local
time.

\section{Spectral Analysis of Fire Blight Symptoms}
\label{secSpectralAnalysis}

% NOTE: Placed here, after the dataset is described but before the

% paired NIR/RGB images and does not depend on the SAM-assisted or

Section~\ref{secSensingPlatform} motivated including a
near-infrared multispectral camera on the basis of prior work
showing red-edge reflectance to be discriminative for fire blight
on apple leaves and for Valsa canker on apple bark.
Here we examine that motivation directly on our own data by
comparing the mean spectral signatures of diseased and healthy bark
across the 25 Ximea bands (Table~\ref{tabXimeaBands}) during the
dormant season.
The comparison draws on 14 annotated NIR images; because it
contrasts average per-class reflectance rather than training a
data-hungry model, this modest labeled subset is sufficient to
characterize the class spectra reliably.
The healthy and diseased regions used here were labeled directly on
the NIR frames, independently of the annotation pipeline described
in Section~\ref{secAnnotation}.

For each annotated image, a mean reflectance spectrum was computed
per class by averaging the demosaiced 25-band response over all
labeled pixels within that class's region;
Figure~\ref{figClassSpectra} shows these signatures averaged across
the 14 images for the diseased (positive) and healthy (negative)
classes.
The healthy bark reflects more strongly than the diseased bark
across the full measured range.
The clearest difference in shape appears across roughly
675--760\,nm, where the healthy spectrum rises much more steeply
toward the near-infrared than the comparatively flat diseased
spectrum.
This region coincides with the windows that prior work found
diagnostic---the 700--850\,nm range reported by Skoneczny et al.\
for fire blight on leaves and the 660--760\,nm band pairs reported
by Kurihara and Yamana for Valsa
canker~\cite{Skoneczny2020DetectionFireBlightAppleHyperspectralLeaf,
Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}---confirming,
on dormant apple bark, that this portion of the spectrum carries
discriminative information and motivating our use of the
near-infrared multispectral camera.

\subsection{NDSI Band-Pair Separation Analysis} \label{secNDSI}

To identify the band pairs that best separate the two classes, we
follow the Normalized Difference Spectral Index (NDSI) analysis of
Kurihara and
Yamana~\cite{Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
For each pair of spectral bands $(\lambda_i, \lambda_j)$ with
$i < j$, the NDSI is computed from a reflectance spectrum $R$ as

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NDSI}(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{R(\lambda_j) - R(\lambda_i)}{R(\lambda_j) + R(\lambda_i)},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R(\lambda)$ is the reflectance at band $\lambda$.
Evaluating this on the per-image mean spectra defined in
Section~\ref{secClassSpectra} yields, for every band pair, a
distribution of NDSI values for the positive class and a separate
distribution for the negative class, each with one sample per
annotated image.
Assuming each class's NDSI distribution is approximately Gaussian,
it is summarized by a mean and standard deviation: $\mu_p, \sigma_p$
for the positive class and $\mu_n, \sigma_n$ for the negative class.
Two statistics are then derived for every band pair.

The first is a Mahalanobis-style \emph{separation}, which quantifies
class separability as

\begin{equation}
    D_M(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{|\mu_p - \mu_n|}{\sigma_p + \sigma_n}.
\end{equation}

\noindent This measures the gap between the class means in units of
pooled within-class standard deviation rather than as a raw
difference, normalizing for the fact that a given mean separation is
more meaningful when the class variance is low than when it is high.
Larger $D_M$ indicates a band pair at which the positive and
negative NDSI distributions are more cleanly separated.
The second is a \emph{classification threshold}, the NDSI cutoff at
which a one-dimensional classifier best separates the two classes,
taken as the point of equal Mahalanobis distance to both class means,

\begin{equation}
    T(\lambda_i, \lambda_j) =
    \frac{\sigma_p \mu_n + \sigma_n \mu_p}{\sigma_p + \sigma_n}.
\end{equation}

\noindent This is a variance-weighted average of the two class
means, biased toward the class with tighter spread rather than a
simple midpoint.
Both statistics were computed across all $\binom{n}{2}$ band-pair
combinations of the $n$ available bands and visualized as heatmaps
indexed by $(\lambda_i, \lambda_j)$, following the presentation of
Kurihara and Yamana.
The separation heatmap identifies the band pairs carrying the most
discriminating power and is used to select the pair for
classification, while the threshold heatmap reports the specific
NDSI cutoff a classifier built on each pair would use.

The results are shown in Figure~\ref{figNDSIHeatmaps}.
The separation heatmap (Figure~\ref{figNDSISeparation}) peaks in a
compact cluster near $\lambda_i \approx 800$\,nm and
$\lambda_j \approx 875$\,nm, identifying that near-infrared band
pair as the most discriminating; the nearest available Ximea bands
are 806 and 877\,nm.
The threshold heatmap (Figure~\ref{figNDSIThreshold}) reports the
corresponding cutoffs, with a broad high-threshold band along a low
$\lambda_i$ near 650\,nm paired with $\lambda_j$ between roughly 775
and 875\,nm.
Both patterns are consistent with the class spectra of
Figure~\ref{figClassSpectra}: the most cleanly separating pair lies
within the near-infrared plateau where the two classes are offset in
magnitude, while the elevated thresholds for pairs bridging the
650\,nm red-edge region and the near-infrared reflect the slope
difference seen there.

Notably, the strongest separation here lies in the near-infrared,
around 800 and 875\,nm, beyond the 660--760\,nm red-edge window that
Kurihara and Yamana identified for Valsa canker and that Skoneczny
et al.\ found discriminative for fire blight on
leaves~\cite{Skoneczny2020DetectionFireBlightAppleHyperspectralLeaf,
Kurihara2022DetectionValsaCankerHyperspectralCanker}.
We attribute this shift to the differences in setting noted in
Section~\ref{secXimeaCamera}: those studies imaged bloom-season
leaves and green bark, whereas our data capture dormant, woody bark,
where the dominant cue is a near-infrared reflectance-magnitude
offset between necrotic and healthy tissue rather than a red-edge
inflection.
The red-edge slope difference nonetheless still contributes, as
reflected in the threshold response for band pairs that span it.
